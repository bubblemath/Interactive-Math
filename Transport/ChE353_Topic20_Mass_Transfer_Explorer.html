Fick's law looks like Fourier's law with different letters — until you notice the second term. That extra term has no counterpart in heat or momentum transfer, and almost everything that makes mass transfer awkward traces back to it.

NA,z = −cDAB dxA/dz + xA(NA,z + NB,z). The first term is pure diffusion: A sliding down its own gradient. The second is A riding along in the bulk motion of the mixture, and it is proportional to how much of the mixture is A. Watch what happens as xA climbs.

DAB, ν and α all have units of length²/time, and all three answer the same question: how far does a disturbance spread in a given time? Their ratios are the Schmidt, Prandtl and Lewis numbers, and the fact that those are near 1 in gases and wildly off 1 everywhere else explains most of what mass transfer feels like.

Companion to the ChE 353 Topic 20 study guide. Ideal-gas mixtures throughout: c = P/RT with R = 8.314 J/mol·K, giving c = 40.9 mol/m³ at 25 °C and 1 atm. Penetration depth uses the conventional δ ≈ √(Dt) scaling — it is an order-of-magnitude statement, not an exact solution, and the exact error-function result puts the 1% penetration at about 3.6√(Dt). The unit conversion is D[ft²/hr] = 3.87·D[cm²/s].
