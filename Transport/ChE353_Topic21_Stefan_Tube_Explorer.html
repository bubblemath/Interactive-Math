Stagnant Film & Stefan Tube Explorer

A liquid evaporating up a tube into a sweep gas. B never moves — yet B has a concentration gradient the whole way up. Resolving that apparent contradiction is the single most useful thing in this topic.

If there were no bulk flow, xA would fall in a straight line up the tube, exactly like temperature across a slab. It does not. The profile sags, and the size of the sag is the size of your error if you forget the bulk-flow term. Crank xA1 up and watch the two curves separate.

Split each species' flux into its two pieces at every height. For A they add up to the same constant everywhere — they must, or A would pile up. For B they cancel exactly, at every single height. B is swimming downward against the current at precisely the speed the current carries it up.

Stop topping the liquid up and the level falls, so the diffusion path gets longer, so evaporation slows down, so the level falls more slowly still. The gap grows as √t — and turning that curve into a straight line is how the Stefan tube becomes a way to measure DAB.

Companion to the ChE 353 Topic 21 study guide. 1 atm throughout, with c = P/RT. Defaults are the guide's acetone problem: pvap = 30.6 kPa at 25 °C so xA1 = 0.302, DAB = 0.11 cm²/s, gap 10 cm, dry sweep gas so xA2 = 0, liquid molar density 1.35×10⁴ mol/m³ from ρ = 784 kg/m³ and M = 58.1 g/mol. The quasi-steady assumption behind the falling-level result is that the gas-phase profile re-establishes itself far faster than the level moves, which holds comfortably here: the gas profile settles in seconds, the level takes hours.
