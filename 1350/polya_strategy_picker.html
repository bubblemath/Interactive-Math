This is the step most people skip: between understanding a problem and grinding through it, Pólya says devise a plan. Every strategy has a tell — a feature in the problem that points to the right tool. Learn to read the tell and you stop reaching blindly for a calculation that may not be the shortest road.

The reflex is to start calculating. Add the whole column, list every case, plug in and grind — that instinct is exactly what Pólya's method interrupts. On several rungs the tempting wrong answer is "just start computing," and it's wrong not because it never works, but because the shape of the problem was pointing at something faster.

Why it matters

Planning is a teachable skill, not a talent

It's the step novices skip

Struggling problem-solvers jump from reading to computing. Naming the plan out loud is the habit that separates them from strong ones — and you can teach it directly.

The tell is transferable

"End known, start unknown → work backward" fires on a K–6 word problem and a college proof alike. Students carry the cue, not a memorized answer.

More than one road is fine

Honoring a correct-but-different strategy, out loud, teaches flexibility. Future teachers who model that raise braver problem-solvers.

Strategy choice never has the single clean answer that a common ratio does, and that's the point — problem-solving is judgment. Read the shape, name the tell, and pick your first move on purpose instead of reaching for the nearest calculation.
