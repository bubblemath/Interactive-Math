Break it down until
only primes remain.

Start anywhere. Split 24 as 4 × 6, or 2 × 12, or 3 × 8 — the tree looks completely different each time, and the leaves at the bottom are always the same primes. That is the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, and once you have those primes in exponential form, counting every divisor of the number takes one line of arithmetic.

three different starts, one destination
the same 24, split three ways
every route ends at 2 · 2 · 2 · 3  =  2³ · 3
and the exponents count the divisors
12 = 22 · 31
a divisor may take 2⁰, 2¹ or 2² — 3 choices
and 3⁰ or 3¹ — 2 choices
(2 + 1)(1 + 1) = 6 divisors  →  1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
✋

Two traps, at opposite ends. The first is stopping early: 24 = 4 × 6 looks like a factorization, but neither 4 nor 6 is prime, so the job is half done. The second is the + 1 in the counting rule. It is there because an exponent may be zero — a divisor of 12 can contain 2⁰, 2¹ or 2², which is three choices, not two. Drop the +1s and you will count 2 divisors for a number that has 6.

Why it matters

The theorem everything else rests on

Uniqueness is what makes it useful

If a number had two different prime factorizations, GCF and LCM would be ambiguous and fractions would not reduce to a single lowest form.

Exponents carry all the information

Once you have 7875 = 3² · 5³ · 7, the divisor count, the GCF and the LCM all follow from those three exponents alone.

This is why 1 is not prime

Allowing 1 as a prime would let you paste 1s into any factorization for free, and uniqueness would collapse instantly.

A factor tree can be drawn a dozen ways and always finishes in the same place, which is a genuinely surprising fact worth pausing on. Split until only primes remain, collect them into exponents, and add one to each exponent to count every divisor the number has.
