in years.

Simple interest is I = Prt — principal, times the annual rate, times the time. And the future value is what you actually have at the end: A = P + I, or A = P(1 + rt) in a single step. The formula is short; the units are where it goes wrong.

✋

The rate is per year, so the time must be in years too. Your notes' $2,040 loan runs for 4 months — which is t = 4/12 = ⅓, giving I = $51. Put t = 4 into the formula and you get $612, twelve times too much, and nothing about the arithmetic complains. Months go over 12 before they go anywhere near the formula. And the rate is a decimal, not a percent. 7.5% enters as 0.075; entering 7.5 makes the answer a hundred times too large. Make both conversions before you multiply anything, and the rest is one line. Finally, I and A answer different questions — how much interest was earned, against how much is now there. Your notes' $3,000 deposit earns $180 and leaves $3,180 in the account.

Why it matters

A formula that is mostly bookkeeping

Units decide the answer

Every part of Prt is easy. Getting the rate into decimals and the time into years is the whole of the difficulty.

Interest is not the total

A loan question usually wants what you repay, not what the interest came to. Read which one is being asked for.

Any of the four can be unknown

I = Prt has four letters, and a problem may leave any one of them blank. It is one equation, rearranged.

Simple interest charges only on the original principal, however long the money sits. Convert the rate to a decimal, convert the time to years, multiply the three together — and then decide whether the question wanted the interest or the total.
