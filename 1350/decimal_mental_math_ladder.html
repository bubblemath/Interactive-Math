Math 1350 · Lesson 11 · mental computation with decimals

Three operations,
three adjustments.

Every mental strategy here works the same way — nudge the numbers until they are friendly, then undo the nudge. What differs is how you undo it, and it differs by operation. Learn “balancing” as one move and you will apply the subtraction version to an addition and be wrong every single time.

✋

Your notes demonstrate all three under separate headings, and separate headings invite exactly the collapse. A student who has met “add 0.03 to both” for 4.63 − 1.97 will reach for the same move on 9.27 + 3.79 and get 10 + 4.52 — too big by 1.46, which is twice the amount added. The three rules are not arbitrary. A sum measures a total, so whatever one number gains the other must lose. A difference measures a gap, so both endpoints may slide together and the gap survives. A quotient measures a ratio, so both must be multiplied. Different invariant, different adjustment — and knowing which invariant you are protecting is what makes the rule memorable rather than arbitrary.

Why it matters

Strategies with reasons attached

Naming the invariant

Total, gap, ratio. Say which one the operation protects and the adjustment follows — no memorising three unrelated rules.

Mental work exposes understanding

A pupil who can only use the written algorithm has learned a procedure. One who can reshape the numbers has understood them.

These are the same laws again

Compatible numbers is commutativity and associativity; balancing a quotient is the Fundamental Law of Fractions. Nothing here is new.

Mental arithmetic with decimals is not a knack — it is a small set of adjustments, each protecting something different. Ask what the operation measures before you nudge the numbers: a total must be kept level, a gap may slide, and a ratio must be scaled.
