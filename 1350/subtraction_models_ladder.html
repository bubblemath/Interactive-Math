Math 1350 · Lesson 4 · models of subtraction

Same difference,
different question.

Four models all compute a − b, and they mean four different things. Something is removed. Two amounts are compared. An unknown amount is added to reach a target. A position moves along a line. A teacher who only knows one of them can only reach the children whose problem happens to match it.

something is removed
Juan had 8 blocks and gave 3 away. How many are left?
Take away
the original pile shrinks
nothing is removed
Juan has 8 blocks. Susan has 3. How many more does Juan have?
Comparison
both piles stay exactly as they are
Both compute 8 − 3 = 5. Only one of them takes anything away.
✋

Comparison is the one that hides. It computes like take-away, so it gets taught like take-away — and then a child who can subtract 8 − 3 with counters is stranded by "how much longer is the 12 cm ribbon than the 5 cm one?" Nothing is being removed from the ribbon. "Cross some out" has nothing to cross out. The second trap is quieter still: "how many more" appears in comparison and missing-addend problems, and the difference is whether that second amount already exists or is a target still to be reached.

Why it matters

One operation, four situations

Children get stuck on structure

A child fluent at take-away can freeze on a comparison problem. The arithmetic isn't the obstacle — the situation is, and only a teacher who sees the difference can name it.

Missing addend is the bridge

Rewriting 8 − 3 = □ as 8 = □ + 3 is the first step toward algebra, and it's also the formal definition of subtraction.

Models pick the materials

Counters for take-away, two towers side by side for comparison, a number line for movement. The model you diagnose decides what you reach for.

Every one of these problems is answered by the same subtraction, and every one of them feels different to a six-year-old. Ask what the situation is doing — removing, comparing, building up to a target, or moving — and the right materials and the right words follow.
