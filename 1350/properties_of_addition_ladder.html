To name the property,
name what changed.

The order changed → commutative. The grouping changed → associative. Nothing was added → identity. The sum stayed inside the set → closure. Four properties, one question. And the two statements below differ by exactly one thing each — which is the whole difficulty.

✋

Parentheses on the page don't mean "associative." Both statements above contain parentheses, so matching on that alone gets one of them wrong every time. Ask instead: did the parentheses move, or did the numbers inside them swap? And watch the other direction too — closure is the property where nothing visibly happens at all, which is exactly why students can never name it.

Why it matters

Properties are permissions, not vocabulary

They license every shortcut

A child who adds 17 + 3 first inside (25 + 17) + 3 is using the associative property. Naming it is how you tell them their method is legitimate, not lucky.

Closure is why arithmetic is safe

You never worry that adding two whole numbers might produce something that isn't one. Subtraction offers no such guarantee — and that's the door to integers.

The contrast teaches both

Addition has all four properties; subtraction has none. Seeing what subtraction lacks is what makes the properties feel like content rather than labels.

These four properties look like terminology to memorize and are really a set of permissions — the reasons rearranging, regrouping, and estimating are allowed at all. Ask what changed: the order, the grouping, nothing, or only your confidence that the answer stays in the set.
