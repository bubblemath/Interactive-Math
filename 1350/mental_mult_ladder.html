Reshape the factors
before you multiply.

A product does not change when you move a factor from one number to the other — halve this, double that, and the answer stays put. That freedom is what mental multiplication runs on. 16 × 25 is not a hard problem; it is 4 × 100 wearing a disguise.

The trap is reaching for the algorithm. It always works, so it never forces you to look at the numbers — and looking is the whole skill. One honest caveat throughout: more than one strategy usually works. 64 × 25 can be thought of as quarters or halved-and-doubled twice. Each rung asks for the best fit for these particular numbers, and where another strategy is also reasonable, the feedback says so.

Why it matters

Estimation is how arithmetic checks itself

Strategies rest on the properties

Doubling and halving is the identity property; compatible numbers is commutativity and associativity; front-end is distribution. The properties stop being vocabulary here.

An estimate catches errors

Knowing 528 × 8 is "a bit over 4,000" means a dropped placeholder zero can never slip past you unnoticed.

Over or under is the real skill

An estimate you know is a floor is worth far more than a number you merely hope is close.

The algorithm is a fallback that always works and never notices anything. Look at the factors first — halve one and double the other, hunt for the pair that makes a hundred, or find the quarters — and most of these products fall out in a single step.
