Math 1350 · Lesson 11 · ratios

To the other part,
or to the whole?

A ratio compares two quantities by division — a : b means a/b, and the three notations are one object. But two counts give rise to three different ratios, and the one you want depends entirely on what the question compared to what.

✋

The whole is never handed to you — it has to be built. Your notes give 12 girls and 15 boys, and the class of 27 appears nowhere in that sentence. So when a question asks what fraction of the class is female, the number needed most is the one number not written down, and students reach for 12 : 15 because 15 is there and 27 is not. Order matters too. A ratio is not a sum or a product — 4 : 5 and 5 : 4 are different comparisons, and the phrase “the ratio of A to B” fixes which comes first. And reducing hides the counts. 4 : 5 might be 4 and 5, or 12 and 15, or 400 and 500 — the ratio survives simplification, but the actual quantities do not.

Why it matters

The comparison that has to be read carefully

Language decides the ratio

“For every 3 there are 8” and “3 out of 8” use the same numbers and mean different things. The arithmetic is easy; the reading is not.

A unit ratio is a rate

180 miles to 3 hours reduces to 60 to 1 — and 60 to 1 is a speed. Rates are ratios with the second term made 1.

Units must match first

Comparing 3 feet to 18 inches means nothing until both are in the same unit — the same discipline as common denominators.

A ratio is a division dressed in different notation, and the only real difficulty is knowing which two quantities are being divided. Read the phrasing before the numbers: “A to B” fixes the order, “out of” signals the whole, and the whole is usually the number you have to work out for yourself.
