Four symbols, four English words: ∪ is or (everything in either), ∩ is and (only the common ones), ′ is not (everything in U that isn't in A), and − is and not (keep the first, remove the second). Say the word, then walk the element lists. That's the whole job.

Two operations look alike and pull opposite ways. Union grows the set; intersection shrinks it to the overlap — swapping them is the most common slip. And order matters where you'd least expect: B − A ≠ A − B, complement is meaningless without a universal set, and the empty set pulls both ways — P ∩ ∅ = ∅ but P ∪ ∅ = P. Every rung here keeps those live.

Why it matters

Operations are the grammar of the whole lesson

Everything downstream uses them

Venn regions, survey problems, and De Morgan's laws are all just these four operations in disguise. Fluency here makes the rest read easily.

Order and context are silent

B−A ≠ A−B and A′ needs a U — nothing in the symbols reminds you. Getting burned on a drill is how the habit sticks.

"And / or / not" is logic

These operations are the set-theory face of logical connectives. Naming the word first ties sets to the reasoning your students already do.

The symbols do only what their English word says — but the word is easy to skip. Say it first — or, and, not, and-not — then walk the lists, and the operations stop trading places.
