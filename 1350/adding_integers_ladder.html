A positive and a negative
cancel.

Every model in this lesson is showing that one fact. Chips call it a zero pair. The charged field neutralises the two marks where they stand. The number line walks one step forward and one step back. The pattern column simply lets you watch it happen, row after row, until the rule is unavoidable.

Four models, one problem: 3 + (−2) = 1.
✋

The different-signs rule has two halves, and the second one gets dropped. Subtract the absolute values — most students manage that. Then give the answer the sign of whichever number had the greater absolute value — that half vanishes. So (−9) + 4 comes out as 5 instead of −5. It is not an arithmetic failure; the subtraction was right. It is a half-remembered rule, which is why the models matter: with nine red chips and four black, you can see that reds are left over.

Why it matters

Four models because children are different

Chips are countable

A child who cannot yet hold “negative four” as an idea can still put out four red counters and pair them off. The abstraction arrives afterwards.

The number line is movement

Adding stops being “combining amounts” and becomes “start here, then travel” — which is the interpretation that survives into subtraction and multiplication.

Patterns let them find it

A rule a child derives from a column of examples is remembered differently from a rule they were handed. Your worksheet is built around that difference.

The rules for adding integers are short enough to memorise and easy enough to half-memorise, which is exactly the danger. Keep a model in reach — pair off the chips, or take the two steps along the line — and the missing half of the rule becomes something you can see rather than something you must recall.
