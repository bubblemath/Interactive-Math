How many
fit inside?

Every division question is that question. 3 ÷ ½ asks how many halves fit into three — and the answer is six, which is bigger than the three you started with. Read division that way and “invert and multiply” stops being a trick and becomes the thing that answers it.

Dividing can make things bigger, and this is the exact mirror of the previous ladder's surprise. There, multiplying by less than one shrank a number; here, dividing by less than one grows it — and they are the same fact viewed from either side. The second trap is flipping the wrong fraction. 2/5 ÷ 2/3 becomes 2/5 · 3/2, never 5/2 · 2/3 — only the divisor turns over. Students who learn the phrase without the reason flip whichever fraction they happen to see first, and the missing-factor definition is what fixes it, because it shows exactly which number needed undoing.

Why it matters

The rule everybody chants and nobody explains

“Invert and multiply” is a conclusion

It follows from asking which number the divisor was multiplied by. Taught as a phrase, it is the most-memorised least-understood line in the curriculum.

Measurement is the honest reading

“How many ¾-foot pieces from 4½ feet” is what division problems actually look like, and it makes the growing answer obvious.

Now every operation closes

Integers failed under division. With fractions, all four operations stay inside the system — apart from dividing by zero, which nothing repairs.

Division asks how many of one thing fit inside another, and when the thing being measured out is small, a great many of them fit. Read the sign as “how many fit”, turn over the divisor and only the divisor, and check whether your answer grew or shrank against what the question was asking.
