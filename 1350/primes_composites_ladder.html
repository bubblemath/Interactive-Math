Count the divisors.
Exactly two is the whole test.

A number is prime when it has exactly two distinct divisors — 1 and itself. More than two and it is composite. And a number can have fewer than two, which is where the definition earns its keep.

✋

1 is neither prime nor composite. Students call it prime because “only 1 and itself divide it” — but for 1 those are the same number, so it has one divisor, not two distinct ones. That is not a technicality invented to be annoying: if 1 counted as prime, every number would have endlessly many prime factorisations (12 = 2·2·3 = 1·2·2·3 = 1·1·2·2·3…) and the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic would simply be false. The second trap is how far to test: to check 397 you need only try primes up to 19, because any composite has a prime factor no bigger than its square root.

Why it matters

The atoms of arithmetic

Everything downstream needs them

Prime factorisation, GCF, LCM, reducing fractions, common denominators — all of it starts by breaking numbers into primes.

The square-root test is real economy

Testing 397 means trying eight primes, not 395 numbers. Children find that genuinely surprising, and the reason is worth showing.

The sieve is an algorithm

Eratosthenes found every prime under 100 without dividing once — by crossing out multiples. It is one of the oldest algorithms anyone still teaches.

Primes are defined by a count, not by a feeling for which numbers look indivisible. Ask how many distinct divisors a number has — one, two, or more than two — and each of the three answers has exactly one name.
