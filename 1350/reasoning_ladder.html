Math 1350 · Lesson 1 · inductive vs deductive

Does the reasoning guarantee the conclusion,
or only suggest it?

That one question sorts everything on this ladder. Deduction applies a general rule to a specific case, so a true premise makes the conclusion certain. Induction guesses a general rule from specific cases, so it's only ever likely — however many times it holds. And the rule with teeth: a conjecture is never proven by examples, only disproven by one counterexample.

Conjecture: connect points on a circle with chords and the regions double — 2, 4, 8, 16…
Four confirmations, then 6 points gives 31, not 32. The pattern was never proven — it was only ever a guess.
✋

Confirmation feels like proof, and it isn't. A pattern that holds three, four, five times feels locked in — but no pile of confirming cases ever proves a general claim, while a single counterexample settles it for good. Every rung here is built to break the reflex that "it worked so far" means "it's true."

Why it matters

The reasoning under the mathematics

Pattern-finding is inductive

Every "find the next term" your students do is a guess from examples. Powerful, but a guess — and knowing that is the difference between a conjecture and a theorem.

One counterexample is enough

You don't need to check every case to show a rule is wrong. This is how mathematicians actually kill false claims, and it's teachable to a six-year-old.

Certainty has a source

Deductive proof is why math claims are certain in a way lab results aren't. Future teachers who feel that distinction teach it better.

The circle keeps its warning even after you know the answer: a rule can pass every test you throw at it and still be false. Ask the one question — guaranteed, or only suggested? — and reach for a counterexample before you trust a pattern.
