A digit's value is
the digit times its place.

Every position in a numeral is a power of ten, so the same digit means something different depending on where it sits. That one rule runs the whole lesson — it's why zero has to hold an empty place, and why ten of any base-ten block regroups into one of the next size up.

The digit is not the value:
The 6 isn't worth six — it sits in the hundreds place, so it's worth 600.
✋

Reading the digit instead of its value is the mistake underneath all the others. Say "six" for the 6 in 5,678 and expanded form falls apart. Two more follow from it: dropping a zero collapses the places (508 is not 58), and forgetting to regroup leaves you holding 17 longs when 1 flat and 7 longs would do. Every rung here keeps the digit and its value apart.

Why it matters

This is the idea the rest of the lesson rests on

Place value is what "positional" means

Egyptian and Roman numerals just add symbols up. Hindu-Arabic assigns meaning by position — and that's the difference the whole lesson turns on.

Regrouping is carrying

Ten longs becoming one flat is exactly what "carry the one" does. Children meet the blocks first, and the blocks are why the algorithm works.

Other bases are the same move

In base five, five units make a long. Once regrouping is a habit rather than a rule about ten, every other base stops feeling foreign.

Standard form isn't just one way of writing a number — it's the fully regrouped way, the one with the fewest pieces left over. Read each digit as digit × place, keep zero holding its spot, and trade up whenever you have ten of anything.
