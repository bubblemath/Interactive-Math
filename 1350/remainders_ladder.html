Math 1350 · Lesson 6 · remainders in context

One division.
Four answers.

The division algorithm says every pair of whole numbers gives a unique quotient and remainder: a = bq + r, with 0 ≤ r < b. That part is mechanical. The hard part comes next — because what you write down depends entirely on what was asked.

47 ÷ 5 = 9 r 2
the arithmetic never changes — the answer does
Four questions, four different right answers. Only one of them is “9 r 2”.
✋

The remainder is not the answer — it is information about the answer. Nine taxis will leave two people standing on the pavement, so the honest answer is ten. Nine is right if you asked how many full teams you can make. Two is right if you asked how many are left over. And a fraction is only available when the thing can actually be split — 9⅖ ounces of juice is fine; 9⅖ taxis is not a thing. Read the question before you write the number.

Why it matters

Where arithmetic meets judgment

Children answer “9 r 2” to everything

Because that is what the algorithm produced. Teaching them to look back at the question is the whole point of word problems.

Context decides the form

Buses round up, full boxes round down, leftovers get reported, and splittable things take a fraction. The arithmetic cannot tell you which.

r < b keeps it unique

Without that condition a division would have endless valid answers. It is the clause that makes q and r the quotient and remainder.

The division algorithm hands you two numbers and no instructions about what to do with them. Work out q and r, then go back and read what was actually asked — the leftover, the full groups, one more group, or a share that can be split.
