Three agreements,
four pictures.

Before a/b means anything at all, three things have to be settled: what the whole is, that it is cut into b equal parts, and that a of them are taken. Miss any one and the notation stops being about quantity.

Four models, one number:
the three agreements
1The whole being considered — a pizza, a garden, a cup, an inch.
2The whole is cut into b equal parts. Equal is not optional.
3a of those parts are selected.
✋

There is an invisible operator in every mixed number, and it is a plus sign. 2¾ means 2 + ¾, never 2 × ¾ — your own notes spell it out: 2(3/4) ≠ 2 · (3/4) ≠ 6/4. The notation puts a whole number flush against a fraction with nothing between them, which is exactly how we write multiplication everywhere else in algebra, so the misreading is the notation's fault rather than the student's. The second trap is the word “equal.” A shape cut into four pieces does not show fourths unless the four pieces are the same size, and two fractions cannot be compared at all unless they refer to the same whole.

Why it matters

Where arithmetic stops counting things

Children arrive knowing the words

“Half the cookie”, “cut it into fourths”, “nine out of ten” — the vocabulary is already there, and it is a foothold worth using.

Four models, four kinds of learner

Area shows parts of one thing, sets show parts of a collection, the number line shows a position. A child stuck on one may be fluent in another.

This is where integers ran out

Lesson 8 ended with division escaping the integers. A fraction is precisely the answer that was missing.

A fraction is not two numbers stacked up; it is a single quantity, and which quantity depends entirely on what the whole was. Name the whole, check the parts are equal, count the ones you took — and read a mixed number as the sum it actually is.
