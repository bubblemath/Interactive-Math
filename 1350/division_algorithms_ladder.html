Every one of these methods is doing the same thing: asking how many sixes fit? and taking that many away. What separates them is how big a bite you are willing to commit to — any bite you are sure of, or the biggest possible one, every time.

The missing zero is the classic. In 618 ÷ 6, six goes into six once, and then six goes into one zero times — and that zero has to be written down. Skip it and 103 collapses into 13, an answer eight times too small. It is the same place-value failure as the placeholder zero in multiplication, running in the opposite direction: there you forget to add a zero, here you forget to record one.

Why it matters

Why the alternatives are worth teaching

Partial quotients forgives

A child who cannot see that six goes into 72 twelve times can still take ten sixes, then two more. The method absorbs uncertainty instead of punishing it.

The standard algorithm hides the size

Writing “1” above the 7 in 726 really means one hundred. That compression is what makes it fast and what makes the missing zero possible.

Agreement is diagnostic

Ask a stuck student to redo it with partial quotients. If the bites are right but the standard version was not, you have found a place-value problem, not a division problem.

Long division is not a separate skill bolted on after subtraction — it is subtraction, done in the largest steps you can manage and recorded in columns. Ask how many fit, take them away, and keep track of where each bite belongs.
