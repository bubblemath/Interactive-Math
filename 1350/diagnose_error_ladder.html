A wrong answer is
the output of a rule.

Children rarely make random mistakes. They follow a procedure — reliably, patiently, and incorrectly. Every wrong answer below was produced by a rule the student is applying consistently, which means it can be found, named, and retaught. Your job isn't to mark it wrong. It's to work out what they think they're doing.

The trap is calling it careless. A student who writes 623 − 459 = 236 took the smaller digit from the larger in every single column — that is not carelessness, it's a rule, faithfully executed. Carelessness would be inconsistent. Consistency is the tell, and it's also the good news: a rule can be replaced, but "slow down" gives a child nothing to change.

Why it matters

This is the skill the lesson was actually for

You already know how to add

What you don't yet know is how to look at a wrong answer and see the thinking behind it. That's the part that has to be learned, and it's learnable.

Diagnosis picks the remedy

A carry problem needs blocks and trading. A place-value problem needs a place-value chart. You can't choose the right intervention until you know which error you're looking at.

It respects the child

Assuming a rule rather than carelessness treats the student as a thinker who reached a wrong conclusion — which is almost always what actually happened.

Every error pattern here has been documented in real classrooms for decades, and each one is a child being logical with a faulty premise. Find the rule, say it out loud, and you'll know exactly what to teach next.
