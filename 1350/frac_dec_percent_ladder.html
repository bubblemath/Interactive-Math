The % sign is
an operation.

A percent is a fraction with a fixed denominator of 100 — per centum, per hundred — so n% always means n/100. The sign is not a label you can attach or remove: it means divide by a hundred, and dropping it silently multiplies the number by a hundred.

✋

Almost every error here is the same error, wearing different clothes. 0.3 becomes 3% because there is a 3 in it — but 0.3 = 30/100, which is thirty hundredths, so 0.3 = 30%. The point moves two places, because 100 is 10². And the sharpest case in your notes is 2/3 %. It is not 0.667. A student who computes 2 ÷ 3 has done a division that was never asked for — the expression means two-thirds of one percent, so it is (2/3) ÷ 100 = 2/300 = 1/150, about 0.0067. Two-thirds and two-thirds of a percent look almost identical on the page and differ by a factor of a hundred. Read the % sign as an instruction and none of this happens.

Why it matters

One number, three costumes

The denominator is fixed

Percents are comparable precisely because they all sit over 100. That is the whole reason the notation exists.

Two places, always

Converting either way moves the decimal point two places, because 100 is 10². It is Lesson 10's shift doing a job.

Some are exact only as fractions

33⅓% is exactly one third. Written as 0.333 it is merely close, and a teacher should know the difference.

Fractions, decimals and percents are three ways of writing the same number, and moving between them is one operation in each direction. Read % as “divided by one hundred”, do that division, and every conversion on the page becomes a single shift of the decimal point.
