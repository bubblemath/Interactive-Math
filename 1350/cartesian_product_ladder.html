Every element with every element —
and order is the point.

To build A × B, pair each element of the first set with each element of the second, always taking the first component from the first set. Count them and you get a product: n(A × B) = n(A) · n(B). The grid below is the whole idea — every cell is one pair, and the rows times the columns is the count.

P × S — two pairs of pants crossed with three shirts:
2 rows × 3 columns = 6 ordered pairs
Flip the axes and you get S × P — still 6 pairs, but every one of them is reversed. Same count, different set.
✋

Pairs and products don't commute the way sums do. (a, 1) is not (1, a), so A × B ≠ B × A unless the two sets are identical — even though both have the same number of pairs. And the count is a product, not a sum: with 2 pants and 3 shirts you get 6 outfits, not 5. Adding the sizes is the single most common slip here.

Why it matters

Counting combinations starts here

It's the counting principle

2 pants × 3 shirts = 6 outfits is the same idea your students will meet as the fundamental counting principle. Cartesian product is its set-theory name.

Ordered pairs run through math

Coordinates, functions, and relations are all sets of ordered pairs. "First component from the first set" is a habit worth forming now.

Order-matters is a real distinction

Union and intersection don't care about order; products do. Knowing which operations commute is part of understanding what an operation is.

Everything else in this lesson treats a set as an unordered bag — and then the Cartesian product arrives and the order inside each pair carries all the meaning. First component from the first set, every element with every element, and multiply to count.
