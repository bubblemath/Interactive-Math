Distance is never negative.
The sign outside still is.

Absolute value measures how far a number sits from zero, so |−4| and |4| are both 4 — the arcs are the same length. But a minus sign outside the bars is untouched by any of that, which is why −|−3| is −3 and not 3.

“−x” is not a negative number. The definition says |x| = −x when x < 0, and that line looks like it produces something negative — but it is the line that makes absolute value positive. If x = −4, then −x = −(−4) = 4. The minus in −x is an instruction to take the opposite, not a claim about the answer's sign. Your worksheet flags the companion problem in its own margin: negative and subtract are different concepts sharing one mark, and children who hear “minus five” instead of “negative five” inherit the confusion from us.

Why it matters

Where arithmetic stops being about amounts

Integers need a direction

Whole numbers answer “how many”. Integers also answer “which way” — and every model in this lesson is a way of drawing that second question.

The notation is genuinely overloaded

One mark means negative, subtract, and take the opposite. Naming which job it is doing is a habit worth building before the operations begin.

Absolute value drives the rules

Every addition rule in this lesson is stated in terms of absolute values, so a shaky grasp here breaks everything downstream.

Absolute value is not “make it positive” — it is a distance, and distances happen to be positive. Work from the inside out, decide what each minus sign is doing before you touch it, and remember that on the negative side of zero, further from zero means smaller.
