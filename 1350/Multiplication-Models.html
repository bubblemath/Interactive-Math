Math 1350 · Lesson 5 · models of multiplication

Five pictures,
one product.

Repeated addition, an array, an area, jumps along a number line, and a list of pairings — all five give you 4 · 5 = 20, and all five answer a different question about what multiplication means. A teacher who knows only one of them can reach only the children whose thinking happens to match it.

Every one of these is 20. None of them is about the same thing.
✋

Two traps hide behind the commutative property. This lesson defines a · b as b added a times, so 5 · 4 is five 4s — write 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 instead and you still reach 20, from the wrong structure. And an array counts discrete things you could pick up, while an area measures continuous space you could not. Same rectangle on the page; different mathematical object underneath.

Why it matters

One operation, five ways of seeing

Models are the teaching

Your worksheet says it outright: the goal is an arsenal of techniques, not the skill of multiplying. The models are the arsenal.

Each one grows differently

Area survives the jump to fractions and decimals. Repeated addition does not. Knowing which model scales tells you which to lean on.

They explain the properties

Turn an array a quarter turn and you have proved commutativity. Cut it in two and you have proved the distributive property.

The same twenty objects can be five groups of four, a rectangle, a distance, or a menu of pairings. Ask what the situation is really doing — grouping, arranging, measuring, moving, or pairing — and the model that fits will be the one your student can see.
