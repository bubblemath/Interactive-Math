Math 1350 · Lesson 10 · fractions to decimals

Divide,
or build a ten.

Every fraction is a division waiting to happen, so long division converts anything at all. But when the denominator is made only of 2s and 5s, there is a quicker route — scale it up into a power of ten and read the answer straight off. The skill is knowing which one is in front of you.

✋

To balance the denominator, multiply by whichever base you have fewer of. Your notes' example is 6/(2³·5⁴): a power of ten needs equal numbers of 2s and 5s, and here there are four 5s but only three 2s — so you are short one 2, and you multiply top and bottom by 2. Students look at the bigger exponent, see the 5⁴, and multiply by 5, which only makes the imbalance worse. The second trap is counting the places. 205/10,000 is 0.0205, not 0.205 — the denominator demands four decimal places and the numerator supplies only three digits, so a zero has to be written in.

Why it matters

Where the termination rule comes from

Building explains the rule

The next ladder states which fractions terminate. This one shows the mechanism — and a rule you have watched work is a rule you can rebuild.

Division never fails

Whatever the denominator, a ÷ b produces a decimal. Knowing you always have that route makes it safe to try the quicker one first.

Remainders are the next lesson

The chain of remainders in long division is what decides whether a decimal stops or repeats, and it is already visible here.

Two methods, and the choice between them takes one glance at the denominator. Factor it: if only 2s and 5s appear, balance them into a power of ten and read the digits off; if anything else is there, divide — and expect the answer never to stop.
