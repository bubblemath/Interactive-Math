r/n and nt —

Compound interest adds the interest to the principal each period, so the next period earns interest on the interest. The formula packages that up as A = P(1 + r/n)nt — where r/n is the rate for one period and nt is how many periods there are.

✋

Dividing the rate and forgetting to multiply the periods is the standing error. Your notes' $10,000 at 8% for five years, compounded quarterly, needs r/n = 0.02 and nt = 20, giving $14,859.47. Use 0.02 with an exponent of 5 and you get $11,040.81; use 0.08 with an exponent of 20 and you get $46,609.57. Both look like careful arithmetic and both are wrong. Read the two numbers off separately — the rate for one period, and the number of periods — and the formula becomes P(1 + rate)periods. And present value divides. To end up with $10,000 you must deposit less than $10,000 today; if your answer is bigger than the target, you multiplied where you should have divided.

Why it matters

Interest on the interest

The base moves every period

Simple interest charges on the original sum forever. Compounding shifts the base each time, which is the idea that ran through the whole percent half of this lesson.

Frequency helps, but modestly

Monthly beats quarterly on the same money and rate — by $38.99 over five years on $10,000. Real, and smaller than most people expect.

Backwards is the useful direction

“What must I put in now?” is the question people actually face, and it is the same formula rearranged.

Compounding is repeated multiplication by one fixed number, and the formula is bookkeeping for how many times. Work out the rate for a single period and the number of periods, raise and multiply — and round to the nearest cent only at the very end.
