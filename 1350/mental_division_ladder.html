Math 1350 · Lesson 6 · mental division & estimation

Break the dividend.
Never the divisor.

Mental division runs on one move: cut the dividend into pieces you can divide easily, then add the results. It is the distributive property again — but this time it works on one side only, and that asymmetry is where division stops behaving like multiplication.

splitting the dividend works
4256 ÷ 7 ↓ cut 4256 into 4200 and 56 4200 ÷ 7  +  56 ÷ 7 ↓ 600 + 8 = 608
and 7 × 608 = 4256 ✓
splitting the divisor does not
240 ÷ 12 ↓ cut 12 into 10 and 2 240 ÷ 10  +  240 ÷ 2 ↓ 24 + 120 = 144 the real answer is 20
off by a factor of seven — and it is the
natural thing to try, which is why it is worth drilling
the tell points to the strategy
splits at a place boundary → break up the dividend splits into two known multiples → compatible numbers divisor is 10, 100, 1000 → shift the places divisor is 4, 8, 16 → halve repeatedly a fact you already know → use the inverse divisor factors neatly → divide in turn
✋

Division is not symmetric, and that is the whole lesson. a ÷ (b + c) is not a ÷ b + a ÷ c, even though a · (b + c) really is ab + ac. There is one thing you may legitimately do to a divisor: take it apart multiplicatively and divide by each factor in turn — 240 ÷ 12 = 240 ÷ 3 ÷ 4 = 20. Splitting a divisor by addition breaks it; splitting it by multiplication is fine.

Why it matters

Estimation is how you catch a long-division error

The asymmetry is a real idea

Multiplication distributes over addition on both sides; division only over the dividend. Meeting that squarely is worth more than another page of long division.

A quick estimate catches the missing zero

If you know 618 ÷ 6 is “about 100”, an answer of 13 cannot survive. Estimation is the check that makes the algorithm safe.

Knowing the direction is better than knowing the number

An estimate you know is a floor tells you something. A number you merely hope is close tells you nothing.

Everything here is the distributive property, used carefully and on the correct side. Cut the dividend into friendly pieces, divide each, and add — and if you must touch the divisor, factor it rather than split it.
