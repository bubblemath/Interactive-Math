One digit
before the point.

A number is in scientific notation when it reads a × 10ⁿ with 1 ≤ a < 10 and n a whole number. That bound is not decoration — it is what makes the form unique, so that every number has exactly one scientific notation and two of them can be compared at a glance.

✋

All four of your notes' examples are true statements; only two are in scientific notation. −83.2 × 10² really does equal −8320 — there is nothing incorrect about it. It simply is not the form being asked for, because 83.2 is not less than 10. Students read the question as “is this right?” rather than “is this in the form?”, and answer yes to all four. The second trap is that there are two different minus signs here. In −1 × 10⁷ the minus makes the number negative and the exponent is positive; in 7.342 × 10⁻⁶ the minus is on the exponent and the number is positive. Your notes' −10,000,000 is the pairing students least expect: a negative number with a positive exponent.

Why it matters

A form worth being strict about

Strictness buys uniqueness

Because the coefficient must sit in [1, 10), every number has exactly one scientific notation — which is what lets you compare two numbers by glancing at their exponents.

Some numbers cannot be written out

The electron's mass has thirty leading zeros. Nobody counts those reliably, and no calculator screen shows them.

The exponent carries the size

Once a number is in this form, its magnitude is a single integer. That is the entire reason scientists use it.

Scientific notation is not a shorthand so much as a normal form. Put exactly one non-zero digit before the point, count how far the point travelled, and let the sign of the exponent record which way it went — right for negative, left for positive.
