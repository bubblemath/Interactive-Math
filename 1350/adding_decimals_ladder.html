Math 1350 · Lesson 11 · adding & subtracting decimals

Line up the points,
not the ends.

Tenths can only be added to tenths and hundredths to hundredths — so the columns have to be made to match before a single digit is added. Lining up the decimal points is not a new rule to learn; it is the way of guaranteeing that like places meet, and everything else is the whole-number algorithm you already have.

✋

Right-aligning is what worked for whole numbers, and it is exactly what fails here. Line up 2.16 and 1.7 by their last digits and the 7 lands in the hundredths column — you have silently turned 1.7 into 0.17, and the answer comes out 2.33 instead of 3.86. The size check catches it instantly: 1.7 is more than one, so the total must be more than 3.16. An answer smaller than the number you started with cannot be right. And whole numbers hide their point. In 5 + 0.23 the 5 is 5.0, never 0.5 — the decimal point sits after the last digit, invisible precisely when it matters most.

Why it matters

Where the algorithm comes from

The models justify the rule

Coins and blocks make “like with like” obvious before the vertical method is ever mentioned — so the alignment arrives as a conclusion rather than an instruction.

Money is the model children own

Nobody adds three dimes to three pennies and calls the result six of anything. The trap that survives on paper does not survive in coins.

Estimation is the safety net

A pupil who checks whether the answer is plausible catches the alignment error without knowing they made one.

Adding decimals is the whole-number algorithm with one extra requirement placed in front of it. Line up the decimal points, fill the gaps with zeros, add or subtract the columns as usual — and then ask whether the answer is the size it ought to be.
