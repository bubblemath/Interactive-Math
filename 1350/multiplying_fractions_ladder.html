Math 1350 · Lesson 9 · multiplying fractions

“Of” means
multiply.

Cut a square one way for the first fraction and the other way for the second. The overlap is the product — and the algorithm falls straight out of the picture, because the overlap is a × c cells out of b × d. No common denominator, no rebuilding. This is the easiest operation in the lesson and the hardest to believe.

½ of ⅗
⅗ across ½ down the overlap
3 cells of 10  →  3⁄10
and the answer got smaller
✋

Multiplying does not always make things bigger. ½ · ⅗ = 3/10, which is smaller than both factors — and every instinct built on whole numbers says that cannot happen. The fix is the word “of”: half of three-fifths is obviously less than three-fifths. State the rule outright — multiplying by anything less than 1 makes a number smaller, and this lesson is the first time that has been possible. The second trap is mixed numbers. 2½ · 2½ is not 4¼: multiplying the wholes and the fractions separately keeps two terms and silently drops the other two. The honest answer is 6¼.

Why it matters

The easiest algorithm, the hardest idea

The procedure hides the meaning

Multiplying across is so quick that students can do it for years without once wondering what it means — which is why the grid comes first here.

“Bigger” stops being automatic

Until fractions, multiplying always grew things. Meeting a product smaller than both its factors is a real conceptual break, and it needs saying out loud.

Cancel before you multiply

5/12 · 9/10 can be done as 45/120 and then reduced, or cancelled first and done in your head. The second is the same law from ladder 2.

Multiplying fractions asks less of you than adding them — no common denominator, no rebuilding, just across the top and across the bottom. What it asks instead is that you believe the answer: read the sign as “of”, and check whether each factor is pulling the number up or down.
