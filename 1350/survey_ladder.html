Fill the center first,
then work outward.

A survey is a Venn diagram with counts in it. The trick is the order: place the innermost region first, then move out, subtracting each overlap so every person lands in exactly one region. Do it in that order and the numbers reconcile; skip it and you double-count.

Fill the center first, then work outward:
The center (6, all three) is placed first; each outer region is a reported total minus the overlaps inside it. A circle total like 26 is never an "only" region.
✋

A reported total is not a region. "26 liked comedies" is the whole circle, not the "only comedies" slice. "At least one" includes the overlap; "only" and "neither" don't. And a pairwise count like "13 liked comedies and dramas" quietly includes the people who liked all three — so subtract the center before you trust it. Start in the middle and these stop biting.

How it works

Climb it, or jump to the rung you need

Rungs 1–4 read a completed diagram; rung 5 finds an overlap; 6–8 do the center-out fill itself; 9 closes the loop with the outside region. Every survey is freshly randomized, so the numbers are never the ones you just memorized.

Why it matters

This is where the whole lesson pays off

Real data lives in regions

Membership, operations, and regions were all building to this: reading actual counts out of overlapping categories. It's the applied end of the lesson.

Order prevents double-counting

Center-out isn't a preference — it's what keeps each person in exactly one region. Teaching the order is teaching the method.

Your students will meet this

"How many liked only X?" is a staple word problem. Future teachers who fill center-out can explain it, not just do it.

The reported totals overlap on purpose, and the only safe way through them is from the inside out. Place the center, subtract outward, and remember the people outside every circle are a region too.
