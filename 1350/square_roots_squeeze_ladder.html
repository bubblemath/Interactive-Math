Two roots,
one symbol.

A square root of a non-negative number a is any x with x² = a — and there are usually two of them. The principal square root, which is what the symbol √a denotes, is the non-negative one. The equation has two answers; the notation names one.

✋

“The square roots of 36” and “√36” are different questions. 36 has two square roots, +6 and −6, because both square to 36. But √36 means the principal one, so √36 = 6 — never ±6. Meanwhile x² = 36 genuinely has two solutions, and you write x = ±6 to say so. That is a convention about the symbol, not a fact about 36 — and it is exactly why the quadratic formula has to write its ± out in full. If the radical already meant both, the sign would be redundant. The squeeze, meanwhile, narrows but never lands. Each round pins down one more decimal place and there is always another round; for a number like √2 it simply never finishes — which is the irrationality of √2 arriving as a procedure rather than a claim.

Why it matters

Estimation before calculation

The perfect squares are the toolkit

Squeezing needs the squares to 225 at your fingertips. Without them there is nothing to squeeze between.

The symbol carries a convention

Deciding that √ means the non-negative root is what makes it a function — one input, one output — and that decision has to be taught, not assumed.

The procedure shows the irrationality

Squeezing √2 produces a new digit every round and never repeats. Watching that happen is more convincing than being told.

A square root is answered by a question — what number, multiplied by itself, gives this? Trap the answer between the perfect squares on either side, then narrow one decimal place at a time; the bounds close in forever without ever meeting.
