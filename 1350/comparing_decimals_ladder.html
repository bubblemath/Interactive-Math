Longer is
not bigger.

Compare place by place, left to right, and stop at the first place where the digits differ. Counting digits is what works for whole numbers and it is exactly what fails here — .345 beats .1474 despite being shorter, and the cure is to pad with zeros until the columns line up.

“More digits means bigger” is imported straight from whole numbers, where it is true. With 345 and 1474 the longer one is larger; with .345 and .1474 it is not, and nothing about the notation warns you. There is a mirror-image error too — deciding 0.4 beats 0.4213 because “tenths are bigger pieces than ten-thousandths.” Both dissolve the same way: write 0.3450 above 0.1474, line up the places, and read left to right until they differ. Repeating decimals need expanding first — you cannot compare 0.35 and 0.351 from their bars, but written out as 0.353535… and 0.351351… the third place decides it.

Why it matters

The misconception that survives schooling

It is the best-documented error there is

“Longer is larger” is among the most studied misconceptions in elementary mathematics, and it persists into adulthood because whole numbers reward it for years first.

Padding is the whole method

Every difficulty in this ladder is solved by writing the zeros in. It is one move, and it makes the comparison a matter of reading rather than judging.

Money hides the problem

Prices always carry two decimal places, so children compare decimals daily without ever meeting unequal lengths — and then meet them for the first time in an exam.

Comparing decimals is reading, not counting. Pad the shorter number with zeros until the columns line up, then move left to right and stop at the first place where the digits differ — that place decides it, and nothing after it matters.
