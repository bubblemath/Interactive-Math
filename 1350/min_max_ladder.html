Math 1350 · Lesson 7 · the relationship between GCF and LCM

Smaller for the GCF.
Larger for the LCM.

Line up the exponents of the two numbers, prime by prime. The GCF takes the smaller of each pair; the LCM takes the larger. Between them they use every exponent exactly once — and that single observation is the entire reason GCF · LCM = ab.

the exponents of 24 and 30, prime by prime
24 = 2³ · 3   and   30 = 2 · 3 · 5
GCF = 21 · 31 · 50 = 6   ·   LCM = 23 · 31 · 51 = 120
6 × 120 = 720   and   24 × 30 = 720
✋

Swapping min and max is the whole failure mode — and it swaps both answers at once, so a student who has it backwards produces the LCM when asked for the GCF and vice versa. The fix is not to memorise which is which but to notice why they must be opposites: the GCF has to divide both numbers, so it cannot take more of a prime than the poorer number has; the LCM has to be divisible by both, so it must take at least as much as the richer one. Look at the last two columns of the table above — min + max equals the sum on every single row. That is the identity, and it needs no memorising at all.

Why it matters

Two ideas that only make sense together

The identity is a shortcut

Euclid finds a GCF in a handful of divisions. Multiply, divide by it, and you have the LCM without ever factorising either number.

It explains relatively prime

If the GCF is 1 the identity forces the LCM to be the whole product — which is why coprime denominators give the worst common denominator.

Opposites, not arbitrary rules

The GCF must divide both, so it cannot be greedy. The LCM must be divisible by both, so it cannot be stingy. Min and max follow from that alone.

Set the exponents side by side and the two operations become one picture: the GCF takes what both can spare, the LCM takes what either demands. Between them they account for every prime factor exactly once, and that is why their product is always the product of the two numbers.
