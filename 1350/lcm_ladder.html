The smallest thing
they both reach.

Count up in threes and count up in fours, and eventually you land on the same number. The first time that happens is the least common multiple — and the word least is doing real work, because 24 and 36 are common multiples of 3 and 4 as well.

Four routes to the same LCM(3, 4) = 12.
✋

Take the larger exponent — and keep the primes that appear only once. This is the GCF rule turned inside out, and the second half is where it bites. With 24 = 2³ · 3 and 30 = 2 · 3 · 5, that 5 lives only in 30. For the GCF it vanished, because min(0, 1) = 0. For the LCM it stays at full strength, because max(0, 1) = 1 — so the answer is 2³ · 3 · 5 = 120, not 24. Anyone who has just learned to discard unshared primes for the GCF will discard them here too, and be wrong by a factor of five.

Why it matters

Where cycles line up

It is the common denominator

Adding thirds and quarters means finding the first length both can measure — the LCM of the denominators, and nothing more mysterious than that.

The unshared primes are the difference

GCF keeps only what is common; LCM keeps everything from both. Seeing the two side by side is what makes either one stick.

Real questions ask for the packs

The LCM of 10 and 8 is 40, but nobody buys 40 hot dogs — they buy four packets. The last step is the one students skip.

Every method here is looking for the first moment two repeating patterns coincide. Count up until they meet, or take the larger exponent of every prime that appears in either number — including the ones that appear in only one.
