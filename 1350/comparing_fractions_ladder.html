Math 1350 · Lesson 9 · comparing & ordering fractions

Bigger denominator,
smaller piece.

You can only compare parts that are the same size. When they are not, either make them match — a common denominator — or use a strategy that never needs one. And be ready for the two places where whole-number instinct goes exactly backwards.

the same numerator, different pieces
one piece each — but cutting the same whole
into more parts makes each part smaller
and negatives run the other way
the cross products are −290 and −286
compare those as written — not their sizes
✋

Two instincts from whole numbers fail here, in opposite directions. The first: 8 is bigger than 3, but 1/8 is smaller than 1/3, because a bigger denominator means the whole was cut into more and therefore smaller pieces. The second: with negatives, further from zero means less. Your notes' hardest pair is −10/13 against −22/29, and the cross products are −290 and −286 — so −10/13 is the smaller. A student who compares 290 with 286 and forgets the signs gets it precisely backwards.

Why it matters

Where the number line fills in

Ordering exposes understanding

A child who can compute 3/4 + 1/8 but thinks 1/8 > 1/3 has an algorithm and no number sense. Comparison is the test that catches it.

Benchmarks are the fast route

Deciding whether a fraction is above or below a half takes one glance and settles most comparisons without arithmetic at all.

Between any two, infinitely many

Nothing sits between the integers 3 and 4. Between 1/4 and 1/3 there are endlessly many fractions — which is the deepest difference from Lesson 8.

Comparing fractions is never about which numbers look bigger; it is about which quantity is further right on the line. Make the pieces the same size, or judge against a benchmark — and when the numbers are negative, remember that further from zero now means less.
