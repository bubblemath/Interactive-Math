Math 1350 · Lesson 11 · multiplying decimals

Ignore the points.
Then count them.

Multiply as though the decimal points were not there, then place the point so the product has m + n decimal places — one for every place in the two factors together. And the reason is not a pattern somebody noticed: it is what happens when you write both numbers as fractions, because 10m · 10n = 10m+n.

✋

The rule from the last ladder is wrong here, and nothing warns you. Adding demanded that the points be lined up; multiplying demands that they be ignored. Two operations, opposite requirements, identical notation. And the sharp case is when the digits run out. Your notes' (0.02)(0.013) gives 2 × 13 = 26 — two digits — but the product needs 2 + 3 = 5 decimal places. Three zeros have to be inserted between the point and the 26, giving 0.00026. Students who count the places correctly still write 0.026, because the counting stops when the digits do. Expect the product to be smaller, too — 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.15, less than either factor, exactly as with proper fractions in Lesson 9.

Why it matters

A rule with a derivation behind it

Fractions explain it

Writing both factors over powers of ten turns the counting rule into an exponent addition — which is a reason, not an observation.

Two rules, one notation

Line up the points to add; ignore them to multiply. Naming the difference out loud is the only defence against applying the wrong one.

Estimation places the point

Getting the digits right and the point wrong is the commonest failure, and a one-second estimate catches it every time.

Multiplying decimals asks for less alignment than adding them, not more. Multiply the digits as whole numbers, add the decimal places of the two factors, and count that many back from the right — inserting zeros if the digits run out before you do.
