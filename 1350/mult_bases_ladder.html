Math 1350 · Lesson 5 · bases two, five & eight

The facts change.
The algorithm doesn't.

Addition in another base only asked you to carry at a different point. Multiplication asks for more: the times table itself changes. Three fours is still twelve things — but written in base five, twelve things is 22₅. The quantity is the same; only the numeral moved.

base-five times table
every entry written in base five
the same twelve things
3 · 4 = 12  in base ten
3 · 4 = 22₅  in base five two groups of five, and two left over
— the quantity never moved, only the writing
✋

Your times table is a base-ten table. Writing 12 in a base-five answer puts a 1 in the fives place and a 2 in the ones place — that is seven, not twelve. The carry is sharper still: 4 · 4 = 16, and sixteen is three fives and one, so you write 1 and carry 3. Carrying 1 out of habit is wrong twice over — wrong digit, wrong carry.

Why it matters

The clearest look at what a times table really is

Facts are not the operation

You know your tables so well they feel like multiplication itself. Building a new one separates the facts you memorised from the operation underneath.

You become the learner again

Working out 3 · 4 in base five is exactly the effort a seven-year-old spends on 3 · 4 in base ten. That memory makes you patient.

Binary is four facts

The entire base-two multiplication table is 0, 0, 0, 1 — which is why computers can multiply with nothing but shifts and adds.

Nothing about multiplying depends on ten: not the columns, not the carrying, not the partial products. Only the facts change — so build the table first, and the algorithm you already know does the rest.
