The biggest thing
they share.

Four ways to find it, and they are genuinely different kinds of idea — one you can hold in your hand, one built from sets, one read off the prime factorizations, and one that is pure algorithm. They always agree, and knowing more than one is what lets you pick the quick route.

Four routes to the same GCF(24, 30) = 6.
✋

Take the smaller exponent — and mind the primes that are missing. With 24 = 2³ · 3 and 30 = 2 · 3 · 5, the GCF is 2¹ · 3¹ = 6: the minimum of each pair. Take the maximum instead and you get 120, which is the LCM, not the GCF. The subtler half is the 5 — it lives in 30 but not in 24, so its exponent in 24 is zero, and min(0, 1) = 0 means 5⁰ = 1 and it drops out entirely. That is what “including 0” is guarding in the definition.

Why it matters

Why four methods and not one

Listing shows what it means

The GCF is literally the largest number in both lists. Children need to see that before any shortcut will mean anything.

Prime factorization scales

Listing every factor of 10,764 is hopeless; comparing exponents is not. The method survives numbers the first one cannot touch.

Euclid needs no factorization at all

It finds GCF(10764, 2300) in four divisions without ever asking what the primes are — which is why computers use it.

The greatest common factor is one idea wearing four costumes, and the costume you choose should depend on the numbers in front of you. Small numbers: list them. Known factorizations: take the smaller exponent of every shared prime. Large or awkward numbers: divide, take the remainder, repeat.
