Math 1350 · Lesson 11 · dividing decimals

Move both,
or move neither.

Make the divisor a whole number by multiplying it by a power of ten — and multiply the dividend by the same power, because a quotient only survives untouched if both parts are scaled together. Then divide as usual, and the point in the answer sits directly above the point in the dividend.

1.2032 ÷ 0.32 — the same shift, applied twice
two decimal places in the divisor, so both numbers move two places
✋

Moving only the divisor divides the answer by a hundred. It is the commonest slip here, and it happens because “make the divisor whole” sounds like an instruction about the divisor alone. The justification is one you already have: 1.2032 ÷ 0.32 is a fraction, and multiplying its numerator and denominator by the same 100 leaves its value unchanged. That is the Fundamental Law of Fractions from Lesson 9 — the move is legitimate, not a trick, and it is legitimate only when both parts get the same treatment. And watch for the divisor that is already whole. Your notes open with 75.45 ÷ 3 for a reason: nothing needs to move at all, and a student who has just learned to shift will shift anyway.

Why it matters

An old law in new clothing

Nothing new is being asked

Scaling both parts of a fraction was learned in Lesson 9. Here it is called “moving the decimal point”, and recognising it as the same move is most of the battle.

Division can grow a number

8 ÷ 0.25 = 32. Dividing by less than one asks how many small pieces fit, and a great many do.

Shifting does not guarantee an end

Making the divisor whole is not the same as making the answer terminate — that is still a question about 2s and 5s.

Dividing decimals is one preparation followed by ordinary division. Count the decimal places in the divisor, multiply both numbers by that power of ten, and divide — with the point in the quotient standing directly above the point in the dividend.
