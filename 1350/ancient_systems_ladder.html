Two questions sort
every system: does position matter,
and what's the base?

Egyptian, Roman, and tally numerals are additive — a symbol is worth the same wherever it sits, so you just add them up. Babylonian, Mayan, and our own are positional — where a digit sits decides what it's worth. Ask those two questions and every system in this lesson comes apart cleanly.

The Mayan third place is 360, not 400. A pure base-twenty system would go 1, 20, 400, 8000 — but the Mayans bent the third place to 18 × 20 = 360 because it sits close to the 365-day year their calendar tracked. Assume 400 and every three-place numeral you read comes out too big. The other trap is quieter: running positional digits together, reading a Babylonian 23 and 2 as "232" instead of 23 × 60 + 2.

Why it matters

The idea the whole lesson was building toward

Positional systems need a zero

If position carries meaning, an empty place must be marked — otherwise 205 reads as 25. Additive systems never face the problem, which is why Roman numerals get by without one.

Bases were choices

Sixty for the Babylonians, twenty for the Mayans, ten for nearly everyone with ten fingers. Base ten is a habit of anatomy, not a law of arithmetic.

Calendars shaped mathematics

The Mayan third place is 360 because a year is about 365 days. Their number system was bent to fit the sky — a reminder that notation serves a purpose.

Every system in this lesson is answering the same question — how do you write down a quantity — and the answers split on one hinge. Additive systems add symbols wherever they fall; positional systems make place do the work, and then they need a zero to hold an empty place open.
