Math 1350 · Lesson 11 · unit rates & comparison

The unit tells you
which way to read it.

A unit rate is a ratio with the second quantity scaled to 1, and once two offers are expressed per the same unit, comparing them is trivial. But the two obvious unit rates are reciprocals of each other — and the rule for which one wins runs in opposite directions.

✋

Both computations are correct, and the rule for reading them is opposite. Your notes' tickets — 12 for $15 against 20 for $23 — give $1.25 and $1.15 per ticket, where lower wins; or 0.8 and about 0.87 tickets per dollar, where higher wins. Both routes name Option 2, but a student who computes tickets-per-dollar and then reaches for “cheaper means smaller” picks Option 1 and is confidently wrong. Read the unit before reading the number. Dollars per ticket is a cost, so less is better; tickets per dollar is a yield, so more is better. And there is a second method entirely — scale both to a common quantity. Sixty tickets would cost $75 one way and $69 the other, with no division at all.

Why it matters

The arithmetic everybody actually uses

Shelf labels are unit rates

Supermarkets print price per 100 g precisely so that different pack sizes can be compared without any arithmetic at all.

The unit carries the direction

Miles per gallon: more is better. Gallons per mile: less is better. Same car, same facts, opposite readings.

Scaling needs no division

Bringing both offers to a common quantity is often quicker mentally, and it gives an independent check on the unit-rate answer.

A unit rate turns any comparison into a glance. Divide to bring the second quantity to one, say the unit out loud — per ticket, per dollar, per litre — and let that unit tell you whether the winner is the larger number or the smaller one.
