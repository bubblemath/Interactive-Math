Same base,
or no.

Percentages of the same quantity add. Percentages of different quantities do not. That is the entire rule — and your notes' five statements are five different ways of testing whether you have it.

✋

Two of the five are true, and one of the true ones looks wrong. Clean City's fines come to 120% of a monthly income, which sets off every “percents cannot exceed 100” alarm — and the statement is correct. Three fines, each a percentage of the same income, and nothing says a fine has to fit inside what you earn. A student who rejects it has learned a rule that does not exist. Juanita is the trap in its purest form. She paid 45% and 48%, the two bills come to $1000, and 45 + 48 = 93 — every number lines up. But those percentages are of $620 and $380, so they cannot be added as though the bills were the same size. The real total is $279 + $182.40 = $461.40, which is 46.14% — nowhere near 93%.

Why it matters

The reasoning behind the arithmetic

Judging is harder than calculating

Deciding whether a claim could be true is a different skill from working out an answer, and it is the one that transfers.

Correct statements must be recognised too

A pupil who marks everything false has not understood anything. Clean City is where that shows up.

Ask what each percent is of

Every error in this ladder is exposed by one question: a percent of what? Same answer twice means they add.

Percentages are not numbers you can pile up — each one is attached to the quantity it was taken from. Before adding two percentages, ask what each is a percentage of; if the answer is the same thing, add away, and if it is not, go back to the actual amounts.
