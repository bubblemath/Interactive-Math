Reduce. Factor.
Then decide.

A fraction terminates exactly when its denominator — in lowest terms — is built from nothing but 2s and 5s. That is not a new fact; it is 10 = 2 · 5 from the exponent ladder, saying that a denominator can only be scaled into a power of ten if it was already made of the right pieces.

three steps, and the first one is the one that gets skipped
21/28 — the denominator looks fatal until you reduce
a fraction in lowest terms terminates ⟺ its denominator is 2m · 5n
✋

“In lowest terms” is the whole rule, not a footnote. Your notes plant the trap deliberately with 21/28: the denominator factors as 2² · 7, that 7 says repeats, and the answer is wrong — because 21 and 28 share the 7, and the fraction is really 3/4. It terminates at 0.75. Applying the test to the fraction as handed to you fails every time it has not been reduced, and as-handed-to-you is how fractions arrive. The second trap is counting places. For 2m · 5n the answer has max(m, n) decimal places, never m + n — the shorter side is topped up to match the longer, and the longer one sets the length.

Why it matters

A rule with a reason attached

Prediction beats computation

Knowing a decimal will never stop, before starting to divide, saves the pupil who would otherwise fill a page looking for the end of 5/24.

Lesson 7 finally cashes out

Prime factorization looked like an exercise in its own right. Here it answers a question about decimals that nothing else can.

Reducing is a habit, not a step

The reduce-first trap is not really about decimals — it is about checking the form of a fraction before applying any test at all.

Whether a decimal stops was settled the moment the fraction was written, by the primes hiding in its denominator. Reduce it, factor the denominator, and look for anything that is not a 2 or a 5 — if there is nothing else there the division will end, and if there is, it never will.
