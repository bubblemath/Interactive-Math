Math 1350 · Lesson 10 · repeating decimals

The bar covers the repeat
and only the repeat.

Two words, and they are not interchangeable. The repetend is the block of digits that recurs; the period is how long that block is. And the bar goes over the repetend exactly — not over the digits that happen to sit in front of it, and never left off altogether.

0.8333… — the anatomy
one digit waits, then a single digit repeats forever
✋

The digit in front gets swept into the bar, and it changes the number. Your notes open with 0.8333…, and that is the right place to start because the 8 sits immediately before the repeat where it is easy to include by accident. Written correctly the repetend is 3 and the period is 1. Written as 0.83 with a bar over both digits it says 0.838383…, which is a different number. And leaving the bar off is not a small omission — 0.272727 without a bar claims the decimal stops there, which is false. One more convention: the repetend is always the shortest block that works, starting as early as it can. Marking 1818 as the repetend of 0.181818… describes the number correctly but names the wrong block.

Repeats found by remainder

Every decimal comes from real long division, and the repeat is detected when a remainder recurs — never by inspecting a floating-point number.

Why it matters

Notation that carries a claim

The bar is a statement

It asserts that these digits go on forever. Misplacing it or omitting it does not make the writing untidy — it makes it false.

Remainders explain everything

Why a decimal repeats, where the repeat starts, and how long it can be are all answered by watching the remainders.

The pre-period is the old rule

How many digits wait before the cycle begins is max(m, n) — the very quantity that counted decimal places for terminating fractions.

A repeating decimal is exact, not approximate — the bar is what makes it so. Divide until a remainder comes back, mark the block between its two appearances, and put the bar over that block and nothing else.
