One proportion,
three unknowns.

Your notes give three forms of percent problem, and they are the same equation with a different slot empty. Fill in what you know, solve for what you don't — and the only real difficulty is reading the sentence to see which is which.

✋

The hard part is identifying the whole, and it is a reading problem rather than an arithmetic one. The whole is the quantity the percent is taken of, and English hides it in different places each time. In “5 is what percent of 20?” the whole is 20; in “12 is 30% of what?” the whole is the thing being asked for; in “45 correct on an 80-question test” it is 80 with no “of” in sight. The reliable tell is the word “of” — whatever follows it is the whole. That is why “12 is 30% of what number” catches people: the smaller number is the part, and the whole is not written down at all. And the sentence itself gives you the equation — part is percent of whole, read straight onto the proportion.

Why it matters

Three problems that are one problem

Memorising three rules is worse

Three separate procedures are three things to confuse. One proportion with three possible unknowns is a single idea that cannot be mixed up.

“Of” marks the whole

Reading is the skill here. Find the word “of”, and the number after it is the quantity everything is measured against.

Backwards is not the same as forwards

Taking 20% off and then adding 20% back does not return you to the start, because the second percent is of a different number.

Every percent question on your notes' page is the same sentence with a different blank: part is percent of whole. Write down the two numbers you have, put them in the right slots, and solve the proportion — the three “types” are one equation seen from three angles.
