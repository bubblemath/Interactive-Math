Same size,
or nothing.

You can add thirds to thirds and quarters to quarters, and that is the whole of it. Thirds and quarters cannot be counted together at all — so every unlike-denominator problem is really one job: re-cut both wholes until the pieces match, and then just count.

2⁄3 + 1⁄4 — the pieces do not match
so re-cut both into twelfths, and then count
✋

2/3 + 1/4 = 3/7 is the most common error in all of fraction arithmetic, and the picture above is why it is wrong: you would be counting three pieces that are not the same size as each other. There is also a check that catches it in one second — 3/7 is less than a half, but 2/3 on its own was already more than a half, so the sum cannot possibly be smaller than the piece you started with. The second trap is stopping too soon. The formula (ad + bc)/bd always works and rarely finishes: for 3/8 + (−7/12) it gives −20/96, which still needs reducing to −5/24.

Why it matters

The operation that needs the whole of Lesson 7

The LCM finally pays off

Least common multiples looked like an exercise in Lesson 7. Here they are the difference between working in twelfths and working in ninety-sixths.

Estimation catches the error

Knowing 2/3 + 1/4 is “a bit under one” makes 3/7 impossible without any calculation at all.

Regrouping is old news

Borrowing a whole to make 4¼ into 3 and 5/4 is exactly the borrowing from Lesson 4, wearing fraction notation.

Every unlike-denominator sum is the same job twice: make the pieces match, then count them. Find the least common denominator, rebuild both fractions with it, add or subtract the numerators only — and check the size of your answer before you write it down.
