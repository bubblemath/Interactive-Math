Add going right —
unless the next one is bigger.

Roman numerals aren't a lookup table, they're a one-line algorithm. Walk the symbols left to right and add each value, with a single exception: if a symbol is smaller than the one immediately after it, subtract it instead. Every conversion in this lesson is that comparison, repeated.

IV is 4, not 6. Reading a numeral as if it were purely additive is the error underneath nearly every miss — it turns XXXIX into 41 and DCCLXXXIX into 791. The second trap runs the other way: only six subtractive pairs exist, so IL for 49 looks sensible but was never Roman. I can only be taken from V and X, X only from L and C, C only from D and M.

Why it matters

A system without place value

The symbols carry the value

In MLXVI the M means a thousand no matter where it sits. That's what "additive" means — and it's the opposite of how our numerals work.

Subtraction was a patch

Writing 4 as IIII is longer than IV. The subtractive pairs are a compression trick bolted onto an additive system — clever, but limited to six cases.

It runs out

3,999 is the ceiling in standard form. A system with no zero and no place value simply can't scale, which is exactly why ours replaced it.

Roman numerals are the clearest possible demonstration of what a numeration system gives up when it has no place value — and reading them fluently takes just one rule applied repeatedly. Compare each symbol to the next: bigger or equal, add it; smaller, subtract it.
