Rather than testing each number for primality, the sieve works by elimination: take the smallest number not yet crossed out, declare it prime, and cross out all of its multiples. Whatever survives has no factors — and must be prime.

Why it works

Two ideas do all the work

Start crossing at p²

Any smaller multiple of p is p × k with k < p, so it has a smaller prime factor and was already crossed out on an earlier pass. Beginning at p² skips work that is already done.

Stop when p² > N

A composite number n must have a prime factor no larger than √n. So once p² > N, every composite has already been caught, and everything still standing is prime.

1 is neither

A prime has exactly two distinct divisors, 1 and itself. The number 1 has only one divisor, so it is neither prime nor composite — it simply sits outside the question.

Eratosthenes was librarian at Alexandria around 240 BC, and this is still essentially how a computer builds a table of primes today. The sieve never tests whether a number is prime — it only ever crosses out multiples, and lets the survivors speak for themselves.
