Math 1350 · Lesson 6 · models of division

How many groups —
or how many in each?

Every division question is one of exactly two questions, and they are not the same one. Measurement tells you the size of a group and asks how many groups there are. Partitive tells you the number of groups and asks how big each one is. Both are answered by the same arithmetic, and a child stuck on one may be perfectly fluent at the other.

measurement
Ms. Sallay puts 18 students into groups of 3. How many groups?
known: 3 in each group
unknown: the number of groups
partitive
18 cookies are shared equally among 3 friends. How many each?
known: 3 groups
unknown: how many in each
Both are 18 ÷ 3 = 6. In one the 6 counts groups; in the other it counts cookies.
✋

The same numbers, the same answer, opposite unknowns. That is why a child who can share cookies fairly may stare blankly at “how many groups of 3?” — dealing objects out one at a time models the sharing question beautifully and does not model the grouping question at all. If you only ever teach one interpretation, half your class is stranded and you will not be able to see why.

Why it matters

One operation, two questions

It explains a specific stuck child

“She can share but cannot group” is a real and common diagnosis — and it is invisible unless you know the two interpretations are different.

Repeated subtraction is long division

Every step of the standard algorithm asks “how many of these fit?” — which makes long division a measurement procedure, whatever the word problem said.

Missing factor is the inverse

Writing 42 ÷ 6 = □ as 6 · □ = 42 turns an unfamiliar division into a multiplication fact the child already knows.

Division is the only one of the four operations that routinely means two different things, and the arithmetic will never tell you which. Ask what the situation hands you — the size of a group or the number of them — and the model, the materials and the words all follow.
