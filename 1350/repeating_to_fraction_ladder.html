Math 1350 · Lesson 10 · repeating decimals to fractions

Shift past one block,
and the tail cancels.

Call the decimal x and multiply by a power of ten large enough to move past exactly one repetend. Both numbers then end in the same infinite tail — so subtracting kills it, and what is left is an ordinary equation with whole numbers on both sides.

✋

A digit in front that does not repeat changes the whole method. With 0.45 repeating you shift once and subtract x. But your notes' 2.345 — where only the 45 repeats — needs two multiplications: first shift past the 3, then shift past a full block as well, and subtract those two from each other. Subtracting 1000x − x instead is the classic error: the tails are out of step by one digit, so nothing cancels and the result is a mess. The denominator that falls out is worth noticing rather than memorising — as many 9s as there are repeating digits, followed by as many 0s as non-repeating ones. For 2.345 that is 990, and 990 is exactly what 1000 − 10 comes to.

Why it matters

The half of the theorem this ladder supplies

Every repeating decimal is a fraction

The subtraction always leaves whole numbers on both sides, so the result is always a ratio of integers. No exceptions, no special cases.

Algebra doing arithmetic's work

An infinite object is handled by naming it, shifting it, and cancelling. It is many students' first sight of algebra being genuinely useful.

The 9s are a consequence

999 is not a rule to remember — it is what 1000x − x leaves behind. Watching it appear is worth more than being told.

Naming the decimal x turns an endless string of digits into something you can do arithmetic with. Shift past exactly one repetend, subtract the multiple that leaves the same tail, and solve — the answer is a fraction every single time.
