Math 1350 · Lesson 5 · the distributive property

Multiply every term inside.
Then learn to read it backward.

One rule — a(b + c) = a·b + a·c — and it is the busiest idea in the lesson. It is how you multiply 13 × 7 in your head, why an area model can be cut in two, and the reason every multiplication algorithm you know produces the answer it does. Read it right to left instead and it becomes factoring.

reach both terms
7 × 10 = 70 and 7 × 3 = 21
70 + 21 = 91 — and 7 × 13 really is 91
reach only the first
7 × 10 = 70, but the 3 was never multiplied
70 + 3 = 73 — the 3 is still sitting there, un-multiplied
and this is why the algorithm works
14 · 23 = (10 + 4)(20 + 3)
= 10·20 + 10·3 + 4·20 + 4·3
= 200 + 30 + 80 + 12 = 322
✋

The trap is stopping after the first term. 4(10 + 2) = 40 + 2 feels finished because a multiplication happened — but the 2 never got multiplied. The same slip in disguise sits at the top of this ladder: expanding (x + y)(z + w) as just xz + yw, hitting the firsts and lasts and skipping the middle. Every term inside the parentheses has to be reached.

Why it matters

The property that does the most work

It joins two operations

Every other property talks about one operation at a time. This one says how multiplication and addition get along — which is why it shows up everywhere.

Every algorithm is built on it

Partial products, the area box, the lattice, the standard algorithm: all four are the same distribution, arranged differently on the page.

Backward, it is factoring

Children meet ab + ac = a(b + c) years later as algebra. It is the same statement they used to split 13 into 10 and 3.

Splitting a number to make it easier is the oldest trick in mental arithmetic, and the distributive property is the promise that it always works. Reach every term inside the parentheses — and remember the rule reads just as well from right to left.
