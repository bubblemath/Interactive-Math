Every word is an instruction.

An equation is a claim that two amounts are the same. Translating a word problem is three moves and no arithmetic: name the unknown, build each side out of it, and find the word that means “equals.” This is the one ladder where nothing ever computes — the answer is always an expression or an equation, and the moment you would start solving, the rung is already over. It is the step that comes before all the others.

The engine of every word problem

One sentence, taken apart

Take “five less than twice a number is thirteen.” Name the unknown, build each piece, find the hinge — and the equation assembles itself. Watch the third row: the order flips.

“a number”name itx
“twice a number”build2x
“five less than that”order flips2x − 5
“is”the hinge=
“thirteen”build13
2x − 5 = 13

Three moves, no solving. Naming the unknown gives you an x. Building turns each phrase into an expression. The hinge word — “is” — is where the two sides meet. Assemble those and you have the equation. Whether x turns out to be 9 is a separate skill for a different day; this ladder ends the moment the equation is written.

✋ “Less than” and “subtracted from” reverse the order.

“Five less than a number” is x − 5, never 5 − x — the number you hear first is written second. “A number subtracted from 10” is 10 − x. Addition and multiplication forgive you because they commute; subtraction and division punish you every time, and this is where most translation errors actually live.

Second: you choose what x is — pick the quantity everything else is measured from. In “one number is 4 more than another,” name the other one x, so the described one is x + 4. Name the wrong one and every later phrase fights you.

Third: “is,” “are,” “gives,” “results in” — that is the equals sign. Find the verb and everything on each side is one expression. No verb means you have an expression, not an equation — and that is the whole line between arc two and arc three.

Why this matters

This is the rung every other unit leaned on

You already met it four times

Writing the equation for the river, the babysitting fee, the oranges, the percent problem — the hardest step in each was this one. Here it gets drilled on its own, before any solving muddies it.

The solving is the easy part

Once the equation is written, every course before this one taught you how to solve it. The marks are lost at translation, not at arithmetic — so translation is where the practice belongs.

It is a reading skill

No calculator translates a sentence. Deciding what the unknown is, and which phrase means which operation, is a judgment about language — and judgment is what improves with reps.

A word problem is not a wall of arithmetic — it is a sentence with a spine. Name the unknown, build each side out of it, find the verb that joins them. Do that cleanly and the equation writes itself, and everything you already know takes over from there. This ladder stops exactly where your practice on all the others began.
