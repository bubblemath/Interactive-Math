Math 0232 · Unit A2 · multiplying & dividing fractions

Multiplying is a part of a part.

Adding fractions needs a common denominator, because you are combining same-sized pieces. Multiplying and dividing do not. You multiply straight across, and dividing is just multiplying by the flip. The denominator stops being a gate you have to match and becomes one more number to multiply. The whole skill is letting the operation decide the procedure — and not reaching for the common denominator out of habit.

The engine of the whole lesson

The same pair, three operations

Take one half and one third. Adding forces you to match the denominators first. Multiplying and dividing skip that step entirely — and only one of the three operations makes you build a common denominator.

Only adding makes you match. A common denominator is the tool for combining same-sized pieces — and that is the one thing multiplying and dividing never ask for. Multiply the tops, multiply the bottoms, done. To divide, flip the second fraction and you are multiplying again. The operation on the page tells you everything about the procedure.

✋ Don't reach for a common denominator when you're multiplying.

This is an addition rule trespassing on multiplication — the mirror image of the error the arithmetic units kept flagging, where a multiplication rule got applied to addition. 1/2 · 1/3 does not become 3/6 · 2/6. Straight across gives 1/6. The common denominator is a gate for adding, and only for adding.

Second: only the second fraction flips. 2/3 ÷ 4/5 = 2/3 · 5/4. The first fraction stays exactly as it is; only the divisor turns over. Flipping the first, or flipping both, is the reliable division mistake.

Third: a whole number is itself over 1, and dividing by it makes things smaller. 6 = 6/1 and 2 = 2/1. Give a whole number its invisible denominator and the same rule applies. Because dividing by 2 is multiplying by 1/2, the answer shrinks — the reverse of what whole numbers taught you.

Why this matters

The wrong reflex is the one you practiced hardest

Adding taught the wrong habit

Every add-and-subtract problem rewarded finding a common denominator. That reflex is now strong and, for multiplying, wrong. Unlearning it on purpose is the point of this ladder.

Multiplying makes things smaller

A part of a part is less than either part. Half of a third is a sixth. It surprises anyone who learned that multiplication grows things, and holding that surprise in mind catches a lot of errors.

“Of” is everywhere later

Percent of a total, a fraction of a recipe, a share of a whole. Every one is a multiplication. Reading “of” as “multiply” fluently is the same skill the proportion and percent units lean on.

There is only one straightforward rule for multiplying fractions, and division borrows it after a single flip. The hard part is never the arithmetic — it is resisting the common-denominator habit that adding drilled so well. Let the operation decide, multiply straight across, and the whole second half of fractions comes down to one clean move.
