Two negatives don't always agree.

There are two separate sign systems, and the whole trick is never confusing them. When you multiply or divide, you count the negatives — even means positive, odd means negative, and the size of the numbers is irrelevant. When you add or subtract, it's a tug of war — same signs pile up, different signs fight, and the bigger one wins. “Two negatives make a positive” is a multiplication rule. It is simply false for addition.

The engine of the whole lesson

Two systems that don't share a rule

Read down each column. On the left, the sign comes from counting negatives — you never look at how big the numbers are. On the right, the sign comes from comparing sizes — counting negatives tells you nothing. The same two negatives give a positive when multiplied and a negative when added.

The columns don't share logic. In the left column, magnitude is irrelevant — count the negative signs and you are done. In the right column, magnitude is everything — the bigger number decides the sign. The error the whole course kept catching is a student using the left column's rule in the right column's territory.

✋ “Two negatives make a positive” is a multiplication rule.

It is false for addition. −5 + −3 = −8, not +8. Two negatives added pile up into a bigger negative. The “makes a positive” rule only describes what happens when you count negative factors in a product — it says nothing about a sum.

Second: in multiplying, the size doesn't decide the sign; in adding, it's the only thing that does. For times and divide, count the negatives and stop — the magnitudes never touch the sign. For plus and minus, the larger magnitude wins the sign outright. Two opposite logics behind similar-looking symbols.

Third: subtracting a negative is adding. −(−3) = +3. Rewrite every subtraction as “plus the opposite” before the tug of war begins — add a plus and change the sign — or the double negative will flip a sign on you.

Why this matters

The most portable mistake in the course

It contaminates everything

The same sign confusion breaks integers, fractions, combining like terms, and radicals. Fix the regime error once, here, and four later units stop tripping on it.

The sign is its own decision

The size of the answer and the sign of the answer are decided separately — and by different rules depending on the operation. Splitting them is what keeps the two systems from bleeding together.

Counting beats memorizing pairs

You don't need four separate product rules. Count the negative factors: even is positive, odd is negative. One idea replaces the whole table and never fails on a long chain.

Two systems, two logics, one habit to build: read the operation first and decide which regime you are in before you combine anything. Count the negatives when you multiply or divide; run the tug of war when you add or subtract. Keep the two apart and the single most portable error in developmental math simply stops happening.
