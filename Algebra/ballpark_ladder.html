Know the neighborhood before the answer.

Before you compute, you already know three things about the answer: its sign, its rough size, and whether it's even possible. Checking them isn't a feeling — each has a definite answer — and it catches most wrong answers without redoing the arithmetic. Estimate first, then let the exact calculation confirm what you expected. When they disagree, you've found a mistake. This is the ladder that checks all the others.

How it works

One check per rung, and none of them compute

Every other ladder ends in an answer. This one ends one step earlier — at the estimate you check the answer against. Reasonableness is not a vibe: it is three specific checks, each with a definite answer, and running them is what turns a wrong answer from something you submit into something you catch.

03

Just the estimate rung produces a number — and only a ballpark. Every other rung asks for a sign, a size, a range, or a verdict. If you find yourself doing exact arithmetic, you've gone too far.

The engine of every check

Three checks, run as a gauntlet

Take a proposed answer and run it past all three. Each check has a definite verdict. A wrong answer usually fails at least one — and you know it is wrong before you ever recompute.

The estimate is the check. None of this redoes the multiplication. It predicts the answer's neighborhood from what you already know, and when the proposed answer lands outside that neighborhood, the mismatch is the discovery. A good estimate does not hit the answer — it names the range the answer has to live in.

✋ The estimate is the check a calculator can't give you.

A calculator will confidently hand you a wrong answer from one slipped keystroke — it has no idea the result is absurd. Your ballpark is the only second opinion. So when the exact answer misses the estimate badly, the mismatch is not noise to wave away — it is the estimate doing its job. Trust the ballpark and recheck; never trust the calculator over your own prediction.

Second: “multiplying makes it bigger” is only true above 1. Multiplying by a fraction or a decimal below 1 makes things smaller; dividing by one makes them bigger. Whole-number experience lies to you here — check the direction of size, don't assume it.

Third: possibility includes type and range, not just size. An answer of 2.5 people fails on type. A percent over 100 of the whole, or a part bigger than its whole, fails on range. Ask not only “is it about the right size?” but “is it even the right kind of thing, and is it allowed to be this big?”

Why this matters

The habit that checks every other unit

It catches the other errors

A receding rate that came out positive, a fraction product that grew, a percent bigger than its whole — every one is caught by a check on this ladder. It is the safety net under everything else you have learned.

The answer is not the goal

A right answer you can't trust is worth little. Knowing an answer is reasonable — that it has the right sign, size, and range — is what makes it usable. Confidence comes from the check, not the calculator.

It's the math you'll actually keep

Long after the procedures fade, the instinct to ask “is that about right?” stays useful for a tip, a budget, a dose, a measurement. This is the most durable skill in the course.

A number is not an answer until you know its neighborhood. Predict the sign, the size, and what's possible; compute; then check the answer against the prediction. When they agree, you can trust the result. When they don't, you have caught a mistake nobody else would have. That habit — estimate first, verify after — is what separates getting an answer from knowing it's right.
