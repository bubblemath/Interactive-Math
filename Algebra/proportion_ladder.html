One machine, four disguises.

This unit looks like four topics, and it is one. A rate is a ratio with units. A percent is a ratio out of 100. A percent problem is a proportion with 100 as a denominator. Every single question here is two ratios set equal, cross-multiplied, and solved. The only real skill is feeding the machine — getting the four numbers into the right four slots, with the units lined up, before you cross-multiply.

The engine of the whole lesson

Four topics, one structure

Read down the column: each row looks like its own topic, and each is really the same proportion underneath. The lab's own progress report lists these as separate skills — they are separate names for one move.

Rate 120 miles / 4 hoursa ratio, with units
Percent 17%= 17 / 100
Proportion x / 4 = 12 / 16two ratios set equal
Percent problem part / whole = % / 100a proportion, with 100

Set two ratios equal, cross-multiply, solve. That is the entire unit. “OF means multiply, IS means equals, WHAT is the unknown” is not a percent rule — it is a recipe for pouring a sentence into the proportion. Once the sentence is a proportion, there is nothing percent-specific left to do.

✋ The units decide the layout.

A proportion is only true if the same quantity sits on top on both sides. 5 oranges / $1.15 = 15 oranges / x lines up oranges over oranges and dollars over dollars, so it works. 5 / 1.15 = x / 15 quietly swaps oranges and dollars and gives a wrong answer that looks perfectly reasonable. Line up the units before you cross-multiply.

Second: convert the percent before you multiply. 25% of 80 is 0.25 × 80, not 25 × 80. The percent sign is an instruction — “divide by 100” — not a number you can multiply with directly. This is the same “the symbol is a rule, not a value” idea the earlier units kept circling.

Third: OF is ×, IS is =, WHAT is x. “5 is what percent of 20” becomes 5 = x · 20, which is 5 / 20 = x / 100. The three key words are just the translation from English into the one proportion this whole unit runs on.

Why this matters

Proportional reasoning is most of applied math

Rates run daily life

Miles per gallon, dollars per hour, price per ounce, dose per kilogram. Every “per” is a rate, and comparing two of them is a proportion. This is the math you actually use after the course ends.

Percent is everywhere and always the same

Tax, tip, discount, interest, concentration, error. All of them are “percent of the whole is the part” with one slot empty. Learn the one proportion and you have learned all of them.

Setup is the whole skill

Any calculator will cross-multiply for you. What it cannot do is decide which number is the whole and which is the part. That decision is the mathematics, and it is exactly what this ladder drills.

Four topics, four names, one machine. A ratio compares. A rate compares things measured in different units. A proportion sets two comparisons equal. A percent is a comparison against 100. Every problem in the unit is the same three moves — line up the ratios, cross-multiply, solve — and once you see that, the only question left on any problem is which four numbers go in the four slots.
