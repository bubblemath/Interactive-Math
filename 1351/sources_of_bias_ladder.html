Every flaw in a study has a name.

Ten rungs covering selection bias, non-response bias, measurement bias, survey design flaws, and confounding variables. Read the scenario — identify the bias — earn the badge.

Selection Bias
The sample is drawn in a way that systematically over- or under-represents part of the population.
E.g., surveying only gym members to learn about the general public's exercise habits.
Non-response Bias
People who don't respond differ systematically from those who do, skewing results.
E.g., a mail survey on customer satisfaction — only very happy or very angry customers reply.
Measurement Bias
The measurement process or instrument consistently produces values that are too high or too low.
E.g., a miscalibrated scale that reads 2 lbs heavier than the true weight.
Survey Design Bias
Leading, loaded, or double-barreled questions push respondents toward a particular answer.
E.g., "Don't you agree that the new policy is harmful?" is a leading question.
Confounding Variable
A third variable is related to both the explanatory and response variables, making causation unclear.
E.g., ice cream sales and drowning rates both rise in summer — the confounder is hot weather.

Selection vs. Non-response — the classic mix-up

Selection bias Who gets included in the sample is the problem. The researcher chose a flawed group to begin with — certain segments of the population were never given a chance to be selected.
Non-response bias Who actually replies is the problem. The sample was chosen correctly, but the people who didn't respond differ from those who did — so the data you receive is skewed.

Quick test: Could everyone in the population have been selected? If no → Selection bias. If yes, but only some responded → Non-response bias.

💡
Confounding tip: Ask "Is there a third variable lurking?" If Variable A and Variable B both depend on Variable C, C is a confounder. Confounders do not make a study useless — they just mean you can't claim causation.
⚠️
Voluntary response: When participation is optional and self-selected (call-in polls, online surveys anyone can take), that's a specific form of selection bias — people with strong opinions are more likely to respond.
