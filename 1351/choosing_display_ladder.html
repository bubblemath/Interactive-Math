The right graph tells the right story.

Ten rungs on matching data type and research question to the best display — bar chart, pie chart, histogram, frequency polygon, stem-and-leaf, dot plot, and ogive. Read the scenario, pick the display.

Bar Chart
Qualitative (categorical) data. Compares frequencies or amounts across distinct, separate categories.
Key: gaps between bars; categories on one axis, counts/percents on the other.
Pie Chart
Qualitative data. Shows each category as a slice — best when the "part of a whole" relationship matters most.
Key: all slices must add to 100%; works poorly with many categories.
Histogram
Quantitative (numerical) data grouped into classes. Shows the shape of a distribution — where values pile up, where they thin out.
Key: no gaps between bars; class boundaries are continuous.
Frequency Polygon
Quantitative data. Connects midpoints of histogram bars with a line — makes it easy to compare two or more distributions on the same axes.
Key: line graph derived from the histogram; x-values are class midpoints.
Ogive (Cumulative)
Quantitative data. Plots cumulative frequency or relative frequency — answers "how many values fall below X?"
Key: always goes up; useful for percentile and median estimation.
Stem-and-Leaf Plot
Small quantitative datasets (roughly 10–50 values). Preserves every raw data value while showing the distribution's shape.
Key: back-to-back stem-and-leaf compares two groups side by side.
Dot Plot
Small quantitative or discrete datasets. Each value gets a dot stacked above a number line — ideal for spotting clusters, gaps, and outliers.
Key: best for small n; becomes crowded for large datasets.
Time-Series Graph
Quantitative data measured over time. A line connecting data points in chronological order — reveals trends, cycles, and seasonal patterns.
Key: x-axis is always time; values connected by line segments.

Quick decision guide

Quantitative over timeReveal trend or seasonal patternTime-series graph
💡
Histogram vs. bar chart: Both use rectangles — but a histogram has no gaps (data is continuous) and a bar chart has gaps (data is categorical). If you can meaningfully reorder the bars, it's a bar chart.
📈
Ogive tip: An ogive answers "less than" questions. It always starts at 0 and ends at 100% (or total n). The curve never goes down.
🔢
Small n rule: For datasets with roughly fewer than 50 values, a stem-and-leaf plot or dot plot preserves the raw data while still showing shape. For larger datasets, use a histogram.
