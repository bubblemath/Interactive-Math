Math 1351 · Lesson 3 · sampling methods

The method of sampling determines what your data can say.

Ten rungs — from identifying the four sampling methods by their definitions, to spotting which method fits a real scenario, to knowing why each method is used. Earn a badge with 4 correct in a row.

Simple Random
Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. Usually done with a random number table or lottery.
Drawing 30 names from a hat of 500 students.
Systematic
Select every k-th member of a numbered list after a random starting point. Simple to execute on an ordered list.
Survey every 10th customer who enters a store.
Stratified
Divide the population into subgroups (strata) by a shared characteristic, then randomly sample from each stratum.
Sample 20 freshmen, 20 sophomores, 20 juniors, and 20 seniors.
Cluster
Divide the population into naturally occurring clusters, randomly select some clusters, then survey everyone in those clusters.
Randomly select 5 classrooms, then survey every student in those classrooms.

Stratified vs. Cluster — the classic mix-up

Stratified — sample PART of each group

You divide into groups (by gender, grade level, etc.) and then randomly select some members from each group. Every group is represented.

Cluster — survey ALL of a few groups

You divide into clusters (by location, section, etc.) and randomly select entire clusters. Only the chosen clusters are surveyed.

The key question

"Are you selecting people from every group (stratified), or are you selecting whole groups to survey completely (cluster)?"

Systematic — the k value

To find k: divide population size by desired sample size. Want 50 from 500? k = 10 → select a random start from 1–10, then every 10th person.

💡
Memory hook: Stratified = strata = layers. You dip into every layer. Cluster = clusters = bunches. You pick whole bunches and survey everyone in them.
⚠️
Convenience ≠ random. Asking volunteers, surveying whoever is nearby, or polling social-media followers is a convenience sample — not one of the four methods above, and prone to bias.
