Euclid's Postulates & Axioms

Ten rungs from Euclid's five postulates and four common notions, through the ruler and protractor postulates, to applying the segment addition and angle addition postulates.

Quick Reference — Euclid's Postulates & Modern Postulates
NameStatementWhat it guarantees
Postulate 1 A straight line can be drawn from any point to any other point Any two points determine a unique line segment
Postulate 2 A finite straight line can be extended continuously in both directions Line segments can always be extended; lines are infinite
Postulate 3 A circle can be drawn with any center and any radius Circles exist for any center point and distance
Postulate 4 All right angles are equal to one another Right angles are congruent everywhere in the plane
Postulate 5 (Parallel) If a transversal makes co-interior angles summing to less than 180°, the two lines meet on that side Equivalent to: through a point not on a line, exactly one parallel line exists
Ruler Postulate Points on a line can be matched 1-to-1 with real numbers (coordinates) Distance = |coordinate₁ − coordinate₂|
Segment Addition If B is between A and C, then AB + BC = AC Segments add when the middle point lies between the outer points
Protractor Postulate Rays from a vertex can be matched 1-to-1 with numbers 0°–180° Angle measure = |reading₁ − reading₂|
Angle Addition If D is in the interior of ∠ABC, then m∠ABD + m∠DBC = m∠ABC Angles add when a ray lies in the interior
Common Notions Things equal to the same thing are equal; equals added/subtracted to equals give equals; the whole is greater than the part Algebraic substitution and comparison principles
💡
Postulate vs. Theorem: A postulate (or axiom) is accepted without proof — it's a starting assumption. A theorem is proved from postulates. Everything in geometry ultimately traces back to Euclid's five postulates.
⚠️
Segment Addition needs "between." AB + BC = AC only when B is between A and C on the segment. If B is not between them, the equation does not hold.
📌
Postulate 5 is special. For over 2,000 years mathematicians tried to prove it from the other four. They couldn't — replacing it produces non-Euclidean geometries (spherical, hyperbolic).
