a:b

Odds count favourable vs. unfavourable — not favourable vs. all.

Ten rungs — first master odds in favour, then odds against, then switch freely between odds and probability. Earn a badge on each rung by getting 4 in a row.

✓ Odds in Favour
n(E) : n(E′)
favourable : unfavourable
(successes : failures)
⚠ Odds Against
n(E′) : n(E)
unfavourable : favourable
(flip the in-favour ratio)
⇄ Convert to Probability
P(E) = n(E) / n(S)
If odds = a : b, then
P(E) = a / (a + b)

How it works

01 / Count

Find n(E) — outcomes where E occurs — and n(E′) — outcomes where it does NOT.

02 / In Favour

Write n(E) : n(E′). Simplify both numbers by their GCD.

03 / Against

Flip the ratio: n(E′) : n(E). Same numbers, reversed order.

04 / Convert

Odds a : b ↔ P(E) = a/(a+b). The denominator is always the total outcomes.

⚠️

Most common mistake: Writing odds as a fraction instead of a ratio. Probability is n(E)/n(S). Odds in favour is n(E) : n(E′) — the denominator is the unfavourable count, NOT the total. For example, rolling a 2 on a die: P(2) = 1/6, but odds in favour = 1 : 5.

Why it matters

🎰

Gambling & betting

Casinos and sportsbooks post odds, not probabilities. Recognising the difference is a critical numeracy skill.

📈

Risk communication

Medical studies often report odds ratios. Understanding odds vs. probability prevents misreading health statistics.

⚖️

Legal contexts

Forensic statisticians present DNA match odds in court. Jurors need conceptual fluency to evaluate such evidence.

🎮

Game design

Drop-rates and loot tables are often defined in odds form. Converting to probability helps players make informed choices.
