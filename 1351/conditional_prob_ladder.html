Math 1351 · Lesson 1 · conditional probability
P(A|B)

New information shrinks your sample space. Recalculate from there.

Ten rungs — read the notation, apply the formula, then read answers from two-way tables. Earn a badge on each rung by getting 4 in a row.

📐 Definition
P(A | B) = P(A∩B) / P(B)
"P of A, given B"
B has already occurred — restrict to B's outcomes.
🔍 Reduced Sample Space
P(A|B) = n(A∩B) / n(B)
Count only outcomes inside B,
then ask how many also satisfy A.
📊 Two-Way Table
row or column → restrict, then read
Find the given condition's row/column.
That total is your new denominator.

How it works

01 / Read the bar

P(A | B) — the bar means "given." B is the condition that has occurred.

02 / Shrink n(S)

Your new sample space is only B's outcomes. Ignore everything outside B.

03 / Count overlap

Count how many outcomes in B also satisfy A. That is n(A∩B).

04 / Divide

P(A | B) = n(A∩B) / n(B). The denominator is n(B), not n(S).

⚠️

Most common mistake: Using n(S) as the denominator instead of n(B). Once you know B occurred, only B's outcomes are possible. For example, drawing from a deck given the card is red: the new sample space is 26, not 52.

Why it matters

🏥

Medical testing

P(disease | positive test) ≠ P(positive test | disease). Confusing these causes misdiagnosis — the base-rate neglect error.

⚖️

Legal reasoning

The "prosecutor's fallacy" swaps P(evidence | innocent) for P(innocent | evidence). Conditional probability prevents this error.

🤖

Machine learning

Naïve Bayes classifiers — spam filters, recommendation engines — are built entirely on conditional probability calculations.

🎲

Game theory

Updating probabilities as new information arrives (Bayesian updating) is the mathematical engine behind optimal decision-making.
