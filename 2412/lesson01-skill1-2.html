Skill 1.2: Factoring

Goal: factor polynomials completely: GCF first, then the right method for the number of terms, including expressions with rational exponents.

Factoring strategy coach

Factor the polynomial one decision at a time. A wrong choice gets a hint; then the coach shows you the reason.

Factor completely:

The strategy

  1. GCF first. Take out the greatest common factor.
  2. Count the terms.
    • Two terms: difference of squares a² − b², or a sum/difference of cubes a³ ± b³.
    • Three terms: perfect square trinomial, or the ac method.
    • Four terms: grouping.
  3. Check every factor to see if it factors again.
  4. Check by multiplying.
A sum of two squares such as x² + 9 does not factor over the real numbers.
Special factoring formulas
Difference of squaresa² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)
Perfect squarea² ± 2ab + b² = (a ± b)²
Difference of cubesa³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²)
Sum of cubesa³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²)

ac-method finder

For ax² + bx + c: find two numbers that multiply to a·c and add to b. Then split the middle term and group.

    Questions to explore

    In the finder, try a = 1, b = 0, c = −9, then c = 9. What changes?

    x² − 9 has the pair −3 and 3 (product −9, sum 0), so it factors as (x − 3)(x + 3). For x² + 9, no pair multiplies to 9 and adds to 0, so it is prime over the real numbers.

    Why take out the GCF first? Try a = 4, b = 8, c = −12 in the finder.

    The finder still works, but the numbers are bigger (ac = −48). Taking out 4 first leaves x² + 2x − 3, which is quick: 4(x + 3)(x − 1).

    How can you tell a trinomial is a perfect square?

    The first and last terms are squares and the middle term is twice their product: 9x² − 30x + 25 has (3x)², 5², and 2(3x)(5) = 30x. So it is (3x − 5)².
