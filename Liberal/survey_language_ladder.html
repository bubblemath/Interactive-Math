Lesson 5 · translating survey questions into sets and regions

Say it in sets first.

Survey problems are asked in English and answered by adding up regions. Everything hinges on the sentence in between. And is the overlap, or is everything in either circle, not is everything else. Get the phrase into regions correctly and the arithmetic is addition; get it wrong and no amount of careful adding will save the answer.

Two phrases sound almost identical and mean different things. “Liked exactly one” is the three outer crescents. “Liked at least two” is the three lenses plus the centre. Between them sits “exactly two,” which is the lenses without the centre — and that centre is the piece students quietly drop. Look at the third and fourth buttons above and notice how little the English changes while the picture changes a lot.

Why it matters

The translation is the hard part

01

The arithmetic is only addition

Once the phrase is a list of regions, the answer is a sum. Nearly every lost mark comes from the wrong list, not the wrong sum.

02

Everyday “or” is exclusive

“Coffee or tea” usually offers a choice. In a survey question it means at least one, so the overlap always counts.

03

Regions carry into everything after

Filling in a diagram from survey totals, and reading counts back out of it, are both written in this language.

Read the sentence. Name the regions. Only then start adding.
