Lesson 9 · negating a conditional statement

The negation of a conditional is not a conditional.

A conditional makes a promise, and there is exactly one way to break it: the first part happens and the second one does not. So denying p → q means asserting that very situation — p ∧ ~q. The answer is an and statement, and no arrangement of “not” inside an if–then can ever do the job.

✋

Here is why no conditional can be the answer. Every conditional is automatically true whenever its first part is false. So “If you build it, they will not come” is true in the very rows where “If you build it, they will come” is also true — namely the ones where you don't build it. Two statements that are true together cannot be negations of each other. The denial has to claim that you built it, and only an and statement does that.

Why it matters

Denying a promise means pointing at the breach

01

It is how claims get refuted

To disprove “if you do this, that follows,” you produce the one case where the first happened and the second did not.

02

The shape changes

A conditional negates into a conjunction. That surprises people, and it is worth expecting rather than rediscovering.

03

De Morgan is next

Crossing a bracket flips a connective there too. This rule is the same idea one step earlier.

Assert the first part. Deny the second. Join them with “and.”
