Lesson 13 · future value at simple interest

Add the interest on, or multiply once.

The future value is the principal with the interest added: A = P + I. Substituting I = Prt and factoring gives A = P(1 + rt), which does the whole thing in one multiplication. Two routes, identical answers — and the choice between them is the difference between two steps and one.

✋

The 1 inside the bracket is the principal itself. Drop it and you have computed the interest, not the total — and rt alone is a small decimal, so the answer comes out around a twentieth of what it should be. The other slip is writing 1 + r + t: on $9,000 at 6.5% for nine months that gives a factor of 1.815 and a future value of $16,335, nearly double the loan. Both errors are visible the moment you look at the factor: it should sit just above 1.

Why it matters

One formula, two shapes

01

The factor is a single number

Once 1 + rt is worked out, any principal can be run through it. That is how a lender quotes a deal.

02

It rearranges easily

With A = P(1 + rt) as one equation, solving for P, r or t is a division rather than a two-stage unwind.

03

The factor is a built-in check

It must sit just above 1. Anything far from that means the rate or the time went in wrong.
