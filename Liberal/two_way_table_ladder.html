Lesson 19 · empirical probability and two-way tables

The denominator is the one in the corner.

Empirical probability counts what actually happened rather than what could happen, but the fraction has the same shape: observed occurrences over total observations. In a survey table the numerator is whichever cells the question describes — and the denominator, unless the question says otherwise, is the grand total in the bottom-right corner, not the row or column the numerator happens to sit in.

✋

A male who prefers action is 10 out of 56, not 10 out of 27. Your worksheet picks one person at random from everybody surveyed, so every one of the 56 is a candidate and the denominator is 56 — giving 5/28. Dividing by the 27 males answers a different question: given that the person is male, how likely are they to prefer action. Both are sensible; only one was asked.

How it works

Why it matters

Same fraction, different source

01

Theory needs no data

A fair die gives 1/6 before anything is rolled. Empirical probability needs the rolls to have happened.

02

More trials, closer agreement

Eight tosses can land far from a half. Eight thousand rarely do.

03

The question sets the denominator

"Pick a person" means everybody. "Pick a male" means the males. Read which one is being picked from.

Count the cells the question names, then divide by whoever could have been picked.
