Lesson 21 · histograms, frequency polygons and stem-and-leaf plots

One data set wearing four coats.

The table, the histogram, the frequency polygon and the stem-and-leaf plot are four ways of drawing exactly the same forty numbers. Nothing is added when you move from one to the next, and only one of them throws anything away. Learn to read across them and a question you cannot answer in one display can often be answered in another — or, just as usefully, shown to be unanswerable there at all.

▊

Histogram bars touch; bar-chart bars do not. A bar chart puts separate categories side by side, so the gaps say "these do not run into one another". A histogram's classes butt up against each other along a number line — 79 is followed immediately by 80 — so the bars do too. The gap, or its absence, is information.

How it works

Climb it, or jump to the rung that trips you up

Rungs 1–4 read a histogram: what it is, what one bar says, which is tallest, and how to add across several. Rungs 5–6 place the frequency polygon, which needs the class midpoint. Rungs 7–8 read a stem-and-leaf plot and connect its leaf counts back to the table. Rung 9 asks what each display keeps and what it loses. Rung 10 reads a back-to-back plot, where one side runs backwards.

01

Nothing is locked

Start at rung 1 or go straight to the skill you need. Rungs stay open whether or not you earned the ones before.

02

Four in a row earns the badge

Answer with the mouse or press 1–4, then Enter for the next question. One miss resets the streak to zero.

03

Read the picture, not the memory

Every figure you need is on screen. The bar heights, the leaves and the axis are the whole of the evidence.

Conventions. Class midpoint is (lower limit + upper limit) ÷ 2, and the frequency polygon puts its point above that midpoint at the height of the frequency. Stems are the leading digits and leaves the final digit, written in increasing order — and on the left side of a back-to-back plot they increase away from the stem, so that row reads right to left.

Why it matters

Choosing a display is choosing what to lose

01

The histogram shows shape

Where the data piles up and how it tails off, at a glance. It cannot tell you a single person's score.

02

The stem-and-leaf keeps everything

It is a histogram on its side built out of the actual digits, so every original value is still recoverable.

03

Back-to-back compares

Two groups against one spine. The side with the longer row at a stem has more of its people there.

If two displays disagree, one of them was drawn wrong — the numbers underneath never changed.
