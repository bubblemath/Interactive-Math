Lesson 9 · De Morgan's laws in logic

Crossing the bracket flips the connective.

When a ~ moves inside a bracket, it lands on both parts — and the connective between them changes. An and becomes an or, and an or becomes an and. Distributing the negation while leaving the connective alone is the single commonest error, and it produces a statement that is not equivalent to anything you started with.

✋

You have already proved this once. In the set lessons, (A ∩ B)′ = A′ ∪ B′ and (A ∪ B)′ = A′ ∩ B′ — the complement of an intersection is the union of the complements. Swap ∩ for ∧, ∪ for ∨, and the complement mark for ~, and you have the logic version word for word. Same law, second notation.

Why it matters

One rule, two notations, and a lot of use

01

Negations get pushed inward

Between this and the conditional rule, any negation can be driven all the way down onto the individual letters.

02

It shows up in code and in search

NOT (A AND B) becomes NOT A OR NOT B in any query language, and getting it backwards changes what you find.

03

Everyday speech gets it wrong

“It's not both cheap and fast” means one of them fails, not that both do. The flip is what the sentence actually says.

The ~ lands on both parts. The connective in between turns into the other one.
