Lesson 1 · average rate of change, linear models, projection

Divide by the gaps, not the dots.

Tuition went from $15,518 to $33,480 — a rise of $17,962. To get the yearly increase you divide that rise by the number of years it took. Nine bars are drawn, eight gaps sit between them, and sixteen years actually passed. Only one of those three numbers belongs under the fraction bar.

✋

The bars are labeled every two years, and that is the whole trap. Each gap between neighboring bars is worth two years, not one, so the number of gaps and the number of years are different numbers. Count the bars and you are off by one. Count the gaps and you are off by a factor of two. Both mistakes produce a clean-looking answer that no amount of careful arithmetic afterwards will fix.

Why it matters

This is slope, three chapters early

01

Rise over run, in dollars

Average rate of change is slope before it gets called slope. Getting the run right here is the same skill you will need with a formula later.

02

The model is only two numbers

A starting value and a rate. Once you can pull both off a graph, writing T = mx + b is bookkeeping.

03

A projection is not a reading

Plugging in a year past the data gives a number, and the number is a guess. Knowing which is which is part of the answer.

Count the years, not the bars. Everything after that is arithmetic you already know.
