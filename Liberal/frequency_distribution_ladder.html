Lesson 21 · sampling and frequency distributions

Trade the list for a count.

A raw list of forty scores tells you everything and shows you nothing. A frequency distribution keeps one column of values and one column of counts, and the counts always add back to the number of items you started with. When the values are too spread out to list one at a time, you group them into classes of equal width and count those instead. Nothing is invented and nothing is lost — the list is just re-arranged into a shape you can read.

✋

The class width is not 49 − 40. For the classes 40–49, 50–59, 60–69 … the width is the distance from one lower limit to the next: 50 − 40 = 10. Subtracting inside a single class gives 9, which is the count of whole numbers minus one, not the width. Ten numbers fit in 40–49, and ten classes of ten cover 0 through 99 exactly.

Conventions. Class width is the difference between two consecutive lower class limits, as your worksheet defines it. Every grouped table here uses classes that are equally wide and butted up against each other with no gaps, so counting the whole numbers in one class gives the same width — both methods agree on every table you will see. At least means "that value or more"; fewer than means "strictly below".

Why it matters

01

The table feeds the picture

A histogram is this table drawn to scale. Get the classes wrong and every bar after it is wrong too.

02

Grouping costs you something

Once a score is filed under 70–79 you can no longer say it was 74. That is the price of a readable table.

03

Who got counted still matters

A tidy table built from a bad sample is a tidy table about the wrong people.

The counts must add back to the number of things you collected. If they do not, stop and recount.
