Lesson 4 · three sets, eight regions, reading and building the diagram

Three circles, eight homes.

Two circles gave you four regions and two questions to ask about each element. Three circles give you eight regions and three questions: is it in A, is it in B, is it in C. Answer all three and the region is decided. Answer only two and you will land an element in the wrong lens every time.

✋

Region V is not “the middle of A and B.” The lens where A and B overlap is split in two by circle C: the part outside C is region II, and the part inside C is region V. An element in A and B and C belongs in V alone — writing it in II as well is the commonest error on this page. And region VIII is still out there, holding everything in U that none of the three circles caught.

Why it matters

Eight names you will use constantly

01

Expressions become region lists

A ∪ (B ∩ C) is just “regions I, II, IV, V, VI.” Saying it that way turns a compound operation into bookkeeping.

02

Two sets are equal when their regions match

That is the whole method behind proving the distributive law and De Morgan's laws later in this lesson.

03

Building is harder than reading

Given rosters, most errors are placement errors, not arithmetic. Region V and region VIII are where they happen.

In A? In B? In C? Three answers pick one region out of eight, and every element gets exactly one.
