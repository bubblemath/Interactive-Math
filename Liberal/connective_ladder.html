Lesson 7 · connectives, and the six English forms of the conditional

The antecedent is not always written first.

Most connectives are easy: and, but, yet and nevertheless are all ∧, and or is ∨. The conditional is the one that costs marks, because English has six ways to say it and three of them put the consequent at the front of the sentence. Reading left to right and writing the arrow in that order gets it backwards.

antecedent — the tail of the arrow consequent — the head of the arrow
✋

Two words do opposite jobs, and they differ by one syllable. A plain if marks the antecedent: “q if p” is p → q. But only if marks the consequent: “p only if q” is also p → q. So the same clause moves to the other end of the arrow depending on which of the two phrases it follows. The first five buttons above all come out as p → q even though the sentences look nothing alike; the sixth changes one word and reverses the whole thing.

Why it matters

Direction is the whole content of a conditional

01

p → q and q → p differ

“If it rains, the game is cancelled” and “If the game is cancelled, it rained” are not the same claim, and only one of them was made.

02

Translation comes next

Every sentence in the next lesson has to be turned into symbols before anything else happens. A reversed arrow poisons everything downstream.

03

Truth tables will expose it

p → q and q → p have different columns. Getting the direction wrong changes the answer, not just the notation.

Find the antecedent first, wherever it sits in the sentence. Then write the arrow away from it.
