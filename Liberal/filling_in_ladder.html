Lesson 5 · building a Venn diagram from survey results

Start at the centre, subtract outward.

A survey never hands you regions. It hands you totals that pile on top of each other: the 13 who liked comedies and dramas already includes the 6 who liked all three. So the centre goes in first, then each lens loses the centre, then each circle loses everything already written inside it. Work in any other order and the numbers you subtract are not yet known.

Writing 13 into the comedy-and-drama lens is the mistake this whole method exists to prevent. That lens is only the people who liked those two and not science fiction, so it holds 13 − 6 = 7. Do that once and everything downstream is wrong: the comedy circle no longer balances, the regions overshoot the number surveyed, and every question you answer afterwards inherits the error.

How it works

Climb it, or jump to the rung that trips you up

Rungs 1–2 set the order and run the easy two-circle case. Rungs 3–6 walk outward one ring at a time: the centre, the lenses, the single regions, the outside. Rungs 7–10 put it together and then test whether you can catch a filling that is wrong, or a survey that could never have happened.

Why it matters

Overlapping totals are everywhere

01

Reported figures overlap

Real survey summaries almost always report totals that share people. Pulling them apart into disjoint pieces is the skill.

02

The diagram checks itself

If the eight regions do not add to the number surveyed, something upstream is wrong, and you can find it.

03

It becomes inclusion–exclusion

The subtractions here are the same corrections that appear later as a formula in counting and probability.

Centre first. Then each lens minus the centre. Then each circle minus everything already inside it. Then the outside.
