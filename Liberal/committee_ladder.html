Lesson 18 · committees with a required make-up

Choose inside each group, then multiply.

When a committee has to contain a set number from each of several groups, the two ideas you already have do the whole job. Within a group, order makes no difference, so each group's count is a plain combination. Between groups, the choices are made one after another, so the counts multiply — the counting principle from Lesson 17, applied to combinations instead of single picks.

✋

Do not put the groups into one pool. Your worksheet's Senate has 100 members, but choosing any 5 of them gives 75,287,520 committees — most of which have the wrong make-up. The requirement of exactly 2 Democrats and 3 Republicans is what splits the problem: 46C2 × 52C3, which is 22,873,500. A required composition is an instruction to count each group separately.

Climb it, or jump to the rung that trips you up

Rungs 9–10 diagnose bad setups and mix everything.

A note on scope. Every quota here is at least one, and every group is drawn from in full or left out entirely. A group nobody is taken from is simply dropped from the multiplication rather than written as a combination, so 0! never appears. Sizes are capped so every product is exact.

Why it matters

Two lessons meeting

01

Within: a combination

The members taken from one group have no roles, so their order never matters.

02

Between: the counting principle

Each way of filling the first quota pairs with every way of filling the second, so the counts multiply.

03

The requirement is the structure

Without a required make-up there is one pool and one combination. With one, there are as many combinations as groups.

One combination per group, multiplied together, and the quotas must add to the size.
