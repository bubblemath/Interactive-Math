Lesson 21 · misleading visual representations

The eye reads area.

A number is a length. Draw it as a picture that grows in two directions at once and the reader sees a square of that length, because area is what the eye actually judges. Lift the bottom of an axis off zero and a small rise becomes a cliff. Space out unequal years evenly and a slowing trend looks steady. None of these change the figures printed on the graph — every one of them changes what the graph says.

◢

43¢ drawn twice over looks like 18¢. In your worksheet's dollar graphic each bill is scaled to its value in height and in width. Shrink both to 43% and the area left is 0.43 × 0.43 = 0.1849 — about eighteen per cent of the original. The label is honest and the picture is not, and the picture is what gets remembered.

Green is honest, red is the effect

A dashed green outline shows the size a figure should have been. Red marks what the drawing leads you to see.

Conventions. Percentages are given to the nearest tenth. A figure scaled in both directions covers an area equal to the square of its ratio — twice as tall and twice as wide is four times the area, not two. "How much taller does it look" is measured from the axis the graph actually draws; "the true change" is measured from zero, where it belongs.

Why it matters

Six questions, asked every time

01

Nothing here is illegal

Every figure on these graphs is correct. The distortion lives entirely in how they were drawn.

02

Whoever drew it wanted something

Ask what case the graph is making, and whether the drawing is doing work the numbers cannot.

03

The arithmetic is your defence

Work the real ratio yourself. If it does not match the impression, the impression was manufactured.

Read the numbers first, then look at the picture and ask what it added.
