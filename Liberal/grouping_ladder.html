Lesson 7 · parentheses, negation scope, and the comma that marks the grouping

The comma is the parenthesis.

A ~ applies to whatever comes immediately after it, and a parenthesis is what extends that to a whole group. Once a statement holds two connectives, the brackets decide which letters belong together — and when the statement is written out in English, the only thing marking that grouping is a comma.

✋

Buttons three and four are word-for-word identical apart from one comma. “If the plant is fertilized then the plant is not watered, and the plant wilts” and “If the plant is fertilized, then the plant is not watered and the plant wilts” contain the same words in the same order; only the comma moves, and it moves the brackets with it. Buttons five and six are your worksheet's own pair, where the comma shifts and the word “if” travels with it. Either way, punctuation is the only cue an exam gives you.

Why it matters

Grouping is not punctuation, it is meaning

01

Two brackets, two statements

The same letters and connectives, bracketed two ways, make two different claims. Only one of them was written.

02

Truth tables need the grouping

The column you build depends entirely on which sub-statement gets evaluated first. A misplaced bracket changes every row.

03

De Morgan is a scope rule

~(p ∧ q) and ~p ∧ q differ for exactly the reason this ladder is about: how far the negation reaches.

Read the ~ as far as the bracket says, and no further. In English, look for the comma.
