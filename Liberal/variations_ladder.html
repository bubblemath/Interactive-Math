Lesson 9 · converse, inverse and contrapositive

Only the contrapositive keeps the meaning.

Three things can be done to p → q: swap the two parts, negate both, or do both at once. Swapping gives the converse, negating gives the inverse, and doing both gives the contrapositive. Only the last of those says the same thing as the statement you started with.

✋

One example settles it. “If you live in Houston, then you live in Texas” is true. Its converse, “If you live in Texas, then you live in Houston,” is false — anyone in Dallas is a counterexample. A true statement and a false one cannot be equivalent, so swapping the parts of a conditional changes what it claims. The contrapositive, “If you do not live in Texas, then you do not live in Houston,” is true right along with the original, and always will be.

Why it matters

Two of these are traps

01

Converse error is everywhere

Treating “all A are B” as though it also said “all B are A” is one of the commonest mistakes in ordinary reasoning, not just in this course.

02

The contrapositive is a tool

Because it always matches, you may prove either one and get the other free. That is used constantly in mathematics.

03

Truth tables settle it

Two of the four columns agree and two agree with each other. Nothing here needs to be taken on trust.

Swap and you get the converse. Negate and you get the inverse. Do both and you keep the meaning.
