Lesson 10 · turning an argument into a single symbolic statement

Premises joined by “and”, the whole thing one conditional.

An argument is not three separate statements to be handled one at a time. To test it you build one statement: all the premises joined by ∧, that whole group as the antecedent, and the conclusion as the consequent. Everything the rest of this lesson does starts from that single formula.

✋

The arrow goes in once, at the very end. Premises are things you are granting, so they pile up with “and” — never with “or”, and never with an arrow between them. Only the step from the granted premises to the conclusion gets an arrow, and it points from the whole group to the conclusion. Writing the conclusion first, or joining a premise directly to it, produces a formula that tests something the argument never claimed.

Why it matters

One formula, then everything else

01

The table needs a single statement

A truth table has one final column. Three separate lines give you nothing to put in it.

02

The shape encodes the claim

“If all of this is granted, then that follows” is exactly what an argument asserts, and the formula says it literally.

03

Standard forms become visible

Once written symbolically, an argument can be matched against the named forms and judged on sight.

Every premise with an “and”. All of them in a bracket. One arrow to the conclusion.
