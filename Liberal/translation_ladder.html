Lesson 7 · English to symbols, and symbols back to English

One letter per statement, one symbol per connective.

Translation is mechanical once the letters are fixed. Find the two simple statements, write the connective between them, and attach each ~ to the letter whose clause actually contains the “not.” The commonest slip is not a misread connective — it is a negation that ends up on the wrong side of the symbol.

p: I eat healthily.    q: I lose weight.
✋

A negated statement does not get its own letter. With p already standing for “I eat healthily,” the sentence “I do not eat healthily” is ~p — not some new letter r. That is what keeps the eight sentences in your worksheet's table down to two letters between them. And it cuts the other way too: when a letter is defined as a negative statement, as in “q: The plant is not watered,” then ~q is the sentence with the “not” taken back out.

Why it matters

Everything downstream reads the symbols

01

A truth table cannot fix a bad translation

The table will be filled in perfectly and answer the wrong question. The error has to be caught here.

02

Two letters, not four

Recognising a negated statement rather than naming it afresh is what keeps a problem small enough to handle.

03

Parentheses come next

Once two connectives appear in one sentence, grouping matters — and grouping is only readable if the letters are right first.

Name the two statements. Write the connective. Put each ~ on the letter whose clause holds the “not.”
