Lesson 19 · theoretical probability

Write the outcomes, then count what qualifies.

Theoretical probability is one fraction: how many outcomes make the event happen, over how many outcomes there are altogether. The arithmetic is never the hard part. The hard part is being sure you have listed every outcome and counted exactly the ones that qualify — which is why writing the sample space down, at least for small experiments, is worth the thirty seconds it takes.

✋

0 and 1 are real answers. Your worksheet asks for the probability of rolling a number greater than 7 on a six-sided die, and for a family of three children to have fewer than four girls. The first is 0 — no outcome qualifies. The second is 1 — every outcome does. Every probability lands between those two, and a result outside them means the counting went wrong, not that the question was strange.

Why it matters

Counting is the whole skill

01

The denominator is fixed first

n(S) depends on the experiment alone. Settle it before you even read what the event is.

02

Wording decides the numerator

"At least one" and "exactly one" pick out different outcomes from the same list, and differ by more than you would guess.

03

Everything later is this

Permutations and combinations arrive to count n(S) and n(E) when listing them is impossible. The fraction never changes.

Count what qualifies, count them all, divide, and reduce.
