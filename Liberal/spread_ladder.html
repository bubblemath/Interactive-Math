Lesson 22 · measures of dispersion

Same mean, different worlds.

Two data sets can share an average and have nothing else in common. The range notices by looking at two numbers, the largest and the smallest. The standard deviation notices by asking every value the same question — how far are you from the mean? — and then averaging the answers. The six steps that follow are the whole of it, and the one students lose marks on is the fifth.

∑

The deviations always add to zero. For your worksheet's 2, 4, 7, 11 the mean is 6, and the distances from it are −4, −2, +1 and +5. Add them: 0. That is not a coincidence about this list — it is true of every list, because the mean is the balance point. So the plain average distance is useless as a measure of spread, and that is why the next step squares them.

Why it matters

An average on its own tells you half the story

01

The range is cheap and fragile

Two numbers decide it. One freak value at either end and the range doubles while nothing else about the data has changed.

02

The standard deviation asks everybody

Every value contributes a distance. Move any one of them and the answer moves — which is why it is worth the six steps.

03

It comes back in the same units

Squaring inflates; the square root at the end undoes it. A spread of 3.92 points is in points, not points squared.

The mean tells you where the data sits. The standard deviation tells you how much you can trust that.
