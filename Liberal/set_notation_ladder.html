Lesson 2 · word description, roster method, set-builder notation

Three ways to name one set.

A sentence, a list in braces, and a rule written with a vertical bar. They look like three different topics and they are three spellings of the same thing. Everything in this lesson gets easier once you can move a set from any one of them into either of the others without stopping to think.

✋

The braces are the set. The rest is packaging. Two rosters with the same elements in a different order are the same set. A letter listed twice counts once. And the set-builder bar reads as such that, not as division or absolute value — everything before it says what the elements are called, everything after it says which ones qualify.

How it works

Climb it, or jump to the rung that trips you up

Rungs 1–2 get the three forms talking to each other and settle where the natural numbers begin. Rungs 3–5 are the endpoints and the ellipsis, where most wrong rosters come from. Rungs 6–10 go both directions, finishing with the empty set and a full three-way translation.

Why it matters

01

Cardinality needs a correct roster

Counting elements is easy. Counting the wrong roster because an endpoint slipped is the actual source of the error.

02

Equal versus equivalent starts here

You cannot decide whether two sets hold the same elements until you can write both of them out.

03

Subsets are element-by-element checks

Every subset question is a comparison of two rosters, so a misread rule quietly breaks the whole comparison.
