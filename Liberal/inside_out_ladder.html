Lesson 4 · nested set operations, the distributive laws

Build the bracket first.

Three sets and two operations give you expressions like A ∪ (B ∩ C). There is no ambiguity about what to do: the parentheses name a set, you build that set, and only then do you use it. Work left to right instead and you will get (A ∪ B) ∩ C, which is a genuinely different answer.

Two of those buttons shade the same picture and one does not. A ∪ (B ∩ C) and (A ∪ B) ∩ (A ∪ C) always agree — that is the distributive law, and it is the reason your worksheet asks for both back to back. But (A ∪ B) ∩ C, which is what you get by ignoring the parentheses and reading left to right, lands somewhere else entirely. Distributing means giving A to both brackets, not moving the parentheses along.

Why it matters

Two laws worth recognizing on sight

01

They look like arithmetic

A ∩ (B ∪ C) = (A ∩ B) ∪ (A ∩ C) is exactly the shape of a(b + c) = ab + ac. The difference is that sets distribute both ways round.

02

Region lists settle any dispute

Two expressions are the same set when they shade the same regions. That check is mechanical and never guesses.

03

Logic uses the same rules

Swap ∪ for “or” and ∩ for “and” and these become the distributive laws of logic, which arrive later this term.

Name the bracket, build it, then use it. If you moved a parenthesis, you changed the question.
